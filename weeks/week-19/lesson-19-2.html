📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
What do citizens owe the republic, and what should they give freely?
Citizenship is not only a list of rights. A republic also asks things of its people. Some of those things the law demands. Others only a good citizen will choose to do.

📚 PART 1 · What the Law Requires and What Virtue Offers13 min
📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary

A few words worth knowing before you read.

civic virtue (SIV-ik VUR-choo)
The habit of putting the common good ahead of your own comfort or gain.
entanglement (en-TANG-gul-ment)
A tie that is hard to get out of, such as a close bond with another country.
naturalization (nach-ur-uh-luh-ZAY-shun)
The legal process by which an immigrant becomes a citizen. Congress sets the rules.
obligation (ob-lih-GAY-shun)
Something the law requires you to do.
responsibility (ree-spon-suh-BIL-ih-tee)
Something a good citizen chooses to do, even though no law forces it.
Selective Service (sih-LEK-tiv SUR-vis)
The government system that keeps a list of men ready to be drafted if the nation ever needs them.

Two Lists

Last lesson asked who counts as a citizen. This one asks what a citizen owes. Think of two lists. The first list is obligationsob-lih-GAY-shunSomething the law requires you to do.. The law requires them, and you can be punished if you skip them. The second list is responsibilitiesree-spon-suh-BIL-ih-teeSomething a good citizen chooses to do, even though no law forces it.. No law forces them. A good citizen takes them up anyway.

The obligationsob-lih-GAY-shunSomething the law requires you to do. are easy to name. Citizens must obey the laws and pay their taxes. They must serve on a jury when they are called. Young men must register with the Selective Servicesih-LEK-tiv SUR-visThe government system that keeps a list of men ready to be drafted if the nation ever needs them.. Children must go to school, and their parents must see that they do. Immigrants who became citizens through naturalizationnach-ur-uh-luh-ZAY-shunThe legal process by which an immigrant becomes a citizen. Congress sets the rules. owe exactly the same duties as people who were born citizens. People who are not citizens must obey the laws and pay taxes too, but only citizens may serve on a jury.

Why does the law ask for these things? A republic cannot run on its own. Someone has to pay for the roads, the courts, and the army. Someone has to sit in the jury box. Taxes and service are the price of owning a share in the common property. A citizen who takes the rights of the republic and refuses the duties is living on other people’s work.

The responsibilitiesree-spon-suh-BIL-ih-teeSomething a good citizen chooses to do, even though no law forces it. are just as important. A citizen should vote and stay informed. A citizen should volunteer, help neighbors, and serve the community. No officer will arrest you for ignoring a news story. Yet a republic whose citizens never read, never vote, and never help is a republic in danger.

Here is a way to see the difference. If you break a law, the state acts against you. If you ignore a responsibilityree-spon-suh-BIL-ih-teeSomething a good citizen chooses to do, even though no law forces it., nobody acts against you. The only thing that suffers is the community. That is why the responsibilitiesree-spon-suh-BIL-ih-teeSomething a good citizen chooses to do, even though no law forces it. are the true test of a citizen’s character. Anyone will do what the law forces. The question is what a person does when the law is silent.

The Jury and the Soldier

Two obligationsob-lih-GAY-shunSomething the law requires you to do. show what a citizen really is. The first is jury service. On a jury, ordinary citizens decide the facts of a case. They share directly in ruling, and they do it in the courthouse, not in the capital. You will see how much that matters when you study the trial of a printer in colonial New York.

The second is military service. In Rome, the soldiers of the legion were citizens who owned land and had a stake in the republic. Earlier in this course you saw the United States use a draft in times of war. The last draft ended in 1973. Men still register with the Selective Servicesih-LEK-tiv SUR-visThe government system that keeps a list of men ready to be drafted if the nation ever needs them., so that a draft could begin again if Congress and the President decided the country needed one.

Notice what these duties have in common. Each one asks the citizen to give time, safety, or money for the common good. A republic is the public thing, so its citizens must share the work of keeping it.

📚 PART 2 · Washington on Virtue12 min

Why a Free People Needs Virtue

Polybius and Cicero both warned that republics decay when citizens care only about themselves. Cicero said that a person is not born for himself alone. Their word for the habit that fights this decay was virtue. Civic virtueSIV-ik VUR-chooThe habit of putting the common good ahead of your own comfort or gain. means putting the common good ahead of your own comfort or gain. Laws can force a duty. They cannot force a person to care.

George Washington agreed. In 1796 he decided not to run for a third term as President. Many Americans would have voted for him again. He could have stayed in office for life, as many rulers did in the old world. Instead he stepped aside and wrote a letter to the people, now called the Farewell Address. He had help with the writing from Alexander Hamilton, but the ideas were Washington’s own, and the people read them in the newspapers.

Think about what it meant to give up power. Most rulers in history held on until they died. A republic needs leaders who can walk away. When a leader does it, the citizens learn that the office belongs to them and not to the man who holds it.

In it he said that religion and morality are “indispensable supports” of political prosperity. He meant that a free government depends on citizens who are honest, who keep their promises, and who feel they answer to something higher than themselves. A people who lose these habits will need a strong ruler to keep order, and then they will no longer be free.

Two More Warnings

Washington gave two other warnings that matter to a citizen. The first was about party spirit. He feared that people would put the winning of their side above the good of the whole country. He thought that anger between parties could open the door to a ruler who promised to end the fighting. You will see how close the country came to that danger in the election of 1800.

The second warning was about entanglementsen-TANG-gul-mentA tie that is hard to get out of, such as a close bond with another country. with other countries. He wrote, “It is our true policy to steer clear of permanent alliances with any portion of the foreign world.” He wanted America to trade with everyone and to bind itself to no one. Later in the course you will see how his advice was followed, and how it was later set aside.

Washington also set an example. Because he left after two terms, later Presidents followed his habit for almost 150 years. Duty and virtue meet here. The law did not force him to go. A good citizen chose to.

Put the two halves of this lesson together. The law asks you for your obligationsob-lih-GAY-shunSomething the law requires you to do., and the republic hopes for your responsibilitiesree-spon-suh-BIL-ih-teeSomething a good citizen chooses to do, even though no law forces it.. A country with only the first would be a machine that works as long as it is watched. A country with both can govern itself.
