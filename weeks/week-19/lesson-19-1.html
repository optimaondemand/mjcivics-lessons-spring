Who gets to be one of the “people” in a republic?

Who Are the People?

In Athens, only some people could take part in ruling. In Sparta and in Rome, too, citizenship was a prize that many people did not hold. Every republic has to answer the same hard question: who are its people? The United States has answered it in different ways at different times. The story is not a straight climb upward. The answer was argued, narrowed, and then widened again.

A citizen is a full member of a country. Citizens have rights, and they owe duties. The Constitution leaves two main roads to citizenship. The first is birth. jus sanguinis (yoos SANG-gwih-nis)
Latin for “right of blood.” The rule that a child takes the citizenship of the parents.
jus soli (yoos SOH-lee)
Latin for “right of the soil.” The rule that a person born in a country is a citizen of it.

Countries have followed two old rules for birth. One is citizenship by place of birth (jus soliyoos SOH-leeLatin for “right of the soil.” The rule that a person born in a country is a citizen of it.). You are a citizen because you were born on the country’s soil. The other is citizenship by parentage (jus sanguinisyoos SANG-gwih-nisLatin for “right of blood.” The rule that a child takes the citizenship of the parents.). You are a citizen because your parents are. The United States mainly follows the first rule. It also gives citizenship to many children born abroad to American parents.

A Color Line in the Law

The first Congress passed a naturalizationnach-ur-uh-luh-ZAY-shunThe legal process by which an immigrant becomes a citizen. Congress sets the rules. law in 1790. It said that only “free white persons” could be naturalizednach-ur-uh-luh-ZAY-shunThe legal process by which an immigrant becomes a citizen. Congress sets the rules.. That phrase told immigrants who were not white that they could not become citizens. For a long time, the nation’s rules about who belonged were tied to race.

Dred Scott, 1857

In 1857 the question reached the Supreme Court. Dred Scott was an enslaved man. His owner had taken him to live in places where slavery was against the law, and Scott sued for his freedom. Chief Justice Roger Taney wrote the Court’s opinion, which is a judge’s written explanation of a decision. Taney said that no Black American, free or enslaved, could be a citizen of the United States.

Taney claimed that the Framers never meant Black Americans to be part of “the people.” Critics answered him right away. The Constitution does not say that citizens must be white. Free Black men had even voted in some states when the Constitution was ratified. Taney’s reading made one group of Americans permanent outsiders in their own country.

The Court went further. It said that Congress had no power to ban slavery in the territoriesTAIR-ih-tor-eeLand that belongs to a country but is not one of its states., where Scott had lived for a time. That undid a compromise that many Americans had trusted to keep the peace. Northerners were stunned. Many Southerners cheered. The Court had meant to settle the fight, and it only made the two sides angrier.

It did not have to go this way. Early on, some of the justices wanted a narrow ruling that would leave the question of citizenship alone. Instead, the Court chose to answer the biggest question it could. A Court with more restraint might have spared the country a decision that made the quarrel over slavery even angrier.

The 14th Amendment, 1868

The decision did not end the argument. It helped push the country toward the Civil War. When the war was over, the nation wrote a new answer into the Constitution. The 14th Amendment was ratified in 1868. Its first sentence says: “All persons born or naturalizednach-ur-uh-luh-ZAY-shunThe legal process by which an immigrant becomes a citizen. Congress sets the rules. in the United States, and subject to the jurisdictionjoor-iss-DIK-shunThe legal power a government or court has over a place or the people in it. thereof, are citizens of the United States and of the State wherein they reside.”

Read that sentence slowly. It sets two roads, birth and naturalizationnach-ur-uh-luh-ZAY-shunThe legal process by which an immigrant becomes a citizen. Congress sets the rules., and it names no race. It wiped out Dred Scott on the question of citizenship. It also made national citizenship come first. A state could no longer say who its people were in a way that shut out the nation’s citizens. The phrase about jurisdictionjoor-iss-DIK-shunThe legal power a government or court has over a place or the people in it. left some gaps. Many Native Americans did not become citizens until a law of 1924.

The same amendment added two more promises. No state may take away life, liberty, or property without due process of law. No state may deny any person the equal protection of the laws. Together with the citizenship sentence, these words became the foundation for much of what the nation did later to protect its people.

👥 Peoples: Immigrants to the United States

Most newcomers in the late 1800s crossed the sea. Many from Europe landed at Ellis Island in New York. Many from Asia landed at Angel Island in San Francisco Bay. Most then settled where jobs and cheap land were, in port cities, mill towns, mining camps, and farm country.

The Amendment Is Tested

Rules written on paper have to survive hard cases. In 1882 Congress passed the Chinese Exclusionex-KLOO-zhunKeeping people out. Act. It stopped Chinese workers from coming to the United States. It also said that Chinese immigrants could not be naturalizednach-ur-uh-luh-ZAY-shunThe legal process by which an immigrant becomes a citizen. Congress sets the rules.. The law showed that a country can write a generous rule for birth and still be stingy about who may join.

The 14th Amendment faced its test in 1898. Wong Kim Ark was born in San Francisco to Chinese parents. After a trip to China, officials refused to let him come home. They said he was not a citizen. The Supreme Court disagreed. It said that his birth on American soil made him a citizen, even though his parents could not be naturalizednach-ur-uh-luh-ZAY-shunThe legal process by which an immigrant becomes a citizen. Congress sets the rules.. The rule of birth held. Wong Kim Ark was one man, but the decision protected every child born here, whatever the nation of the parents.

Lands Beyond the States

Geography adds a twist. The United States holds territoriesTAIR-ih-tor-eeLand that belongs to a country but is not one of its states. that are not states. In 1917 Congress made the people of Puerto Rico citizens by a law. People born in American Samoa are U.S. nationalsNASH-uh-nulA person who owes loyalty to the United States and is protected by it, but is not a full citizen.. They are protected by the United States and carry its passport, but they are not automatically citizens. Their status shows that citizenship is still set by Congress and the courts.

So the republic’s answer to “who are the people?” was not handed down by nature. It was fought over in the courts, settled by war, written into an amendment, and tested again. A citizen today holds something that earlier Americans had to win.
