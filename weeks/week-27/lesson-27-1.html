📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
How does a republic come to an end?
A republic can be conquered from outside. It can also be given away from inside. This lesson follows one that was given away, and the terrible things that followed.

📚 PART 1 · The Republic That Was Given Away14 min

covenant (KUV-uh-nunt)
A solemn agreement or promise, especially between God and a people.
dictator (DIK-tay-ter)
A ruler who holds total power and is not limited by law or elections.
genocide (JEN-uh-side)

hyperinflation (hy-per-in-FLAY-shun)
A very fast rise in prices that makes money almost worthless.

A Republic Born in Defeat

Germany lost World War I in 1918. The Kaiser, its emperor, gave up his throne, and Germans founded a republic. In 1919 they wrote a democratic constitution in the town of Weimar. That is why historians call the new government the Weimar Republic. On paper it was a fine republic. It had elections, a president, and a parliament.

But it started with heavy burdens. Many Germans blamed the new republic for the defeat and for the Treaty of Versailles, which forced Germany to admit guilt and to pay huge sums to the winners. In 1923 the government printed so much money to pay its debts that hyperinflationhy-per-in-FLAY-shunA very fast rise in prices that makes money almost worthless. followed. Prices rose so fast that a loaf of bread cost millions of marks. Families lost the savings of a lifetime. Then came the Great Depression. By 1932 about six million Germans had no work. Frightened people looked for someone to blame, and for someone to save them.

The Road to January 1933

Adolf Hitler led the Nazi Party. He promised work, bread, and national pride, and he blamed Jews, Communists, and the republic itself for Germany’s troubles. In July 1932 the Nazis won about thirty-seven percent of the vote, more than any other party. They did not have a majority. In November they lost some of that support.

The president of Germany was Paul von Hindenburg, an aged war hero who disliked Hitler. In January 1933 a small circle of conservative politicians, led by a former chancellorCHAN-sel-erThe head of the government in Germany, much like a prime minister. named Franz von Papen, urged Hindenburg to name Hitler chancellorCHAN-sel-erThe head of the government in Germany, much like a prime minister.. They believed they could use the Nazis and keep control themselves. On January 30, 1933, Hindenburg agreed.

It did not have to happen. The Nazis were not a majority, and their vote had just slipped. Other roads existed, though none was easy. Hindenburg could have kept a chancellorCHAN-sel-erThe head of the government in Germany, much like a prime minister. from one of the older parties, or he could have refused to hand power to a party that had said it hated the republic. He chose the one that proved fatal. This is a warning about a choice, not about fate.

📚 PART 2 · How a Republic Ends13 min

Using the Rules to End the Rules

🌐 Foreign Language Vocabulary
Kristallnacht (KRIS-tahl-nahkt)
German for “night of crystal.” The name given to the night of broken glass in November 1938.
Reichstag (RYKS-tahg)
German for “diet of the realm.” The parliament of Germany.

Hitler did not need an army to take the republic. He used the republic’s own rules. On February 27, 1933, a fire damaged the building of the German parliament (ReichstagRYKS-tahgGerman for “diet of the realm.” The parliament of Germany.). The Nazis blamed Communists and said the nation was in danger. The next day the president signed an emergency decree that ended freedom of speech, of the press, and of assembly. Thousands of opponents were arrested.

On March 23, 1933, the German parliament (ReichstagRYKS-tahgGerman for “diet of the realm.” The parliament of Germany.) voted on the Enabling Act. It let the chancellorCHAN-sel-erThe head of the government in Germany, much like a prime minister. and his cabinet make laws without the parliament for four years. The act needed two thirds of the vote. Many deputies who would have voted no were already in jail or in hiding, and the act passed. The republic had voted itself out of existence. When Hindenburg died in 1934, Hitler made himself the sole ruler of Germany, a dictatorDIK-tay-terA ruler who holds total power and is not limited by law or elections..

You have read about a similar move in Rome. Augustus kept the old offices, the Senate and the consuls, while holding the real power himself. The forms of a republic can remain long after its substance is gone. In Germany, too, elections and officials went on in name, but there was no longer any way for citizens to say no.

Jews in Europe

Hitler turned his hatred on German Jews. To see what that meant, it helps to know who they were. Judaism is among the oldest religions in the world. Jews believe in one God, who made a covenantKUV-uh-nuntA solemn agreement or promise, especially between God and a people., a solemn promise, with the people of Israel. They follow the Torah, which holds the law and the teachings of Moses, and they keep the Sabbath. Jewish ideas of justice and of the worth of each person helped shape the West, as you have learned.

Jews had lived in Europe for about two thousand years. For much of that time they were treated as outsiders. Kings forced them out of England in 1290 and out of Spain in 1492, and mobs attacked their villages. This hatred is called antisemitisman-tee-SEM-ih-tiz-umHatred of, or prejudice against, Jewish people.. In the 1800s, Jews in Germany gained full rights as citizens. In 1933 about half a million lived there, fewer than one German in a hundred. Many were soldiers, doctors, teachers, and shopkeepers who thought of themselves as Germans first.

You can recognize antisemitisman-tee-SEM-ih-tiz-umHatred of, or prejudice against, Jewish people. by its habits. It blames a whole people for the problems of a country. It repeats stories that Jews secretly control banks or governments. It holds Jews to a standard it holds no one else to. These habits are old, and they appear today as well. A good citizen learns to spot them and to say that they are false.

📚 PART 3 · Citizenship Taken, a People Murdered13 min

From Law to Murder

Step by step, the Nazi government removed German Jews from public life. In September 1935 it passed the Nuremberg Laws. The first said that only people of German blood could be citizens. German Jews, who had been citizens, became mere subjects with no political rights. The second law forbade Jews to marry non-Jews. You read in an earlier lesson that a republic must decide who its people are. Here a government took that power and used it to strip a whole group of its citizenship.

On the night of November 9, 1938, mobs burned synagogues and smashed Jewish shops all over Germany. Broken glass covered the streets, and so the event was named the night of broken glass (KristallnachtKRIS-tahl-nahktGerman for “night of crystal.” The name given to the night of broken glass in November 1938.). Dozens of Jews were killed, and about thirty thousand Jewish men were sent to camps. The police did nothing to stop it.

When World War II began in 1939, the persecution became mass murder. In January 1942, at a meeting at Wannsee near Berlin, officials planned how to carry out the killing of all the Jews of Europe. Jews were sent by train to camps such as Auschwitz. About six million Jewish men, women, and children were murdered. This was genocideJEN-uh-sideThe deliberate killing of a whole people or group.. The Nazis also killed many others, among them Roma, people with disabilities, and political opponents. We call the murder of the Jews the Holocaust.

⚖️ Robert H. Jackson at Nuremberg

Robert H. Jackson was a justice of the United States Supreme Court. President Truman asked him to lead the American prosecution of Nazi leaders. At the trials in Nuremberg in 1945 and 1946, Jackson argued that even the powerful must answer to law. He said that the crimes were so great that civilization could not bear to see them ignored. Many leaders were convicted, and some were hanged.

After the war the world held trials at Nuremberg for the Nazi leaders. The judges heard the evidence in open court, which was the opposite of how the Nazi state had worked. The trials set down a principle that still stands. Following orders is no excuse for committing crimes against humanity.

For Americans, the story has a plain warning. Weimar had a constitution, elections, and courts. It lost its citizens’ loyalty, and its leaders chose to hand power to men who despised it. A republic is kept alive by people who value its rules more than they value winning.
