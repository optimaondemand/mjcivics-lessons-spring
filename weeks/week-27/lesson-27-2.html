September 11 and the American Response

📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
How far should a free republic go to keep itself safe?
On a clear September morning in 2001, the republic was struck. This lesson follows what happened, what the nation did, and the old question of how to protect liberty while defending it.

📚 PART 1 · The Morning of September 1112 min
📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary

A few words worth knowing before you read.

authorization (aw-thur-ih-ZAY-shun)
Official permission to do something.
emergency powers (ih-MER-jen-see POW-erz)
Extra powers a government takes in a crisis.
executive order (ig-ZEK-yuh-tiv OR-der)
A command from the President that has the force of law within the executive branch.
internment (in-TERN-ment)
The locking up of people during a war, without trial, because of who they are.
surveillance (ser-VAY-luns)
Close watching of people or places, often in secret, to gather information.
terrorism (TAIR-er-iz-um)
The use of violence against ordinary people to frighten a country or its government.

The Attacks

On the morning of September 11, 2001, nineteen men hijacked four passenger planes. They were members of al-Qaeda, a group of terroristsTAIR-er-iz-umThe use of violence against ordinary people to frighten a country or its government. led by Osama bin Laden. Two planes struck the Twin Towers of the World Trade Center in New York City. Both towers burned and then fell. A third plane hit the Pentagon, the headquarters of the Defense Department, near Washington, D.C.

The fourth plane, United Flight 93, was headed toward Washington. Passengers learned from phone calls what had happened in New York, and they decided to fight the hijackers. The plane crashed in a field in Pennsylvania. It never reached its target. Nearly three thousand people died that day, among them hundreds of firefighters, police officers, and rescue workers who ran toward the burning buildings.

The Response

The nation moved quickly. On September 18, Congress passed a resolution that gave the President the authorizationaw-thur-ih-ZAY-shunOfficial permission to do something. to use force against those who had planned the attacks. The Taliban, who ruled Afghanistan, had sheltered al-Qaeda, and in October the United States and its allies went to war there. In October Congress also passed the USA Patriot Act. It gave law enforcement more tools to find and stop terroristsTAIR-er-iz-umThe use of violence against ordinary people to frighten a country or its government., including wider powers of surveillanceser-VAY-lunsClose watching of people or places, often in secret, to gather information.. In 2002 the nation created the Department of Homeland Security to bring many agencies together to protect the country.

Ordinary citizens answered the call. Americans lined up for hours to give blood. Volunteers from around the country traveled to New York to help rescue workers and to feed them. Strangers looked after one another. Citizens were not only waiting for the government to act. They acted themselves, as a free people does.

Some Americans also turned their anger on neighbors who had nothing to do with the attacks. Muslim Americans and others who looked like the attackers were threatened and sometimes attacked. On September 17, President Bush visited a mosque in Washington and said that the terroristsTAIR-er-iz-umThe use of violence against ordinary people to frighten a country or its government. did not speak for Islam and that Muslim Americans must be treated with respect. A republic that punishes a whole people for what a few did has failed its own principles. You will see why that matters in a moment.

On September 20, President George W. Bush spoke to Congress and the nation. He called the attacks an assault on freedom itself and promised that the nation would not be frightened into giving up its way of life. His words stated what the republic was for, and what it was willing to defend.

📚 PART 2 · Safety and Liberty14 min

The Old Question

A republic that faces a deadly enemy has to ask how much freedom it will trade for safety. The question was not new in 2001. The Fourth Amendment protects citizens against unreasonable searches and seizures. You read earlier about Terry v. Ohio, in which the Supreme Court decided when a police officer may stop and search a person. After September 11, Americans argued again about where such lines belong. Should the government be able to listen to phone calls or read email without a judge’s order, if it hopes to stop an attack? Supporters of the new powers said that the threat was real, and that officials needed every tool. Critics said that the powers could be abused, and that a free people should not accept being watched. Congress also built some limits into the Patriot Act and set some of its powers to expire unless they were renewed, and the argument continues.

An Earlier Test

The republic had faced this choice before. After the Japanese attack on Pearl Harbor in 1941, many Americans feared that Japanese Americans might help the enemy. In February 1942 President Franklin Roosevelt signed an executive orderig-ZEK-yuh-tiv OR-derA command from the President that has the force of law within the executive branch. that let the army remove people from the West Coast. About 120,000 people of Japanese ancestry, most of them American citizens, were sent to camps. This is called internmentin-TERN-mentThe locking up of people during a war, without trial, because of who they are.. They were not accused of any crime, and no court judged them one by one.

Fred Korematsu, a young American-born man, refused to go and was arrested. In 1944 the Supreme Court, in Korematsu v. United States, upheld the order by a vote of six to three. Several justices said that the army had judged that the need was urgent. The dissenters argued that the order rested on race and not on evidence. Decades later, Congress apologized and paid the survivors, and the Court itself said that the decision was wrong. Liberty had yielded to fear, and the republic had to learn from it.

Rome’s Lesson

The Romans had a name for the same problem. In an emergency, the Republic could name a dictator for a short time. That worked well for a while. But when the general Sulla made himself dictator in 82 BC, he held power far longer than the law allowed, and he used it to kill his enemies. Later generals saw what could be done. Emergency powersih-MER-jen-see POW-erzExtra powers a government takes in a crisis. are easier to give than to take back.

So a free people needs two things in a crisis. It needs the nerve to defend itself, and it needs the sense to keep the law that makes it worth defending. Those who wrote the Constitution knew that courage and restraint must go together. After September 11, Americans showed courage in the towers and on Flight 93. How well the nation kept its liberties as it defended itself is still a matter that citizens debate, and a matter you should study with care.

A republic has to be able to defend itself, and it has to remain a republic while it does. September 11 tested the nation’s courage and its judgment. The lessons of Rome, Weimar, and the internment camps teach that emergency powers can outlast the emergency, and that citizens must stay watchful.
