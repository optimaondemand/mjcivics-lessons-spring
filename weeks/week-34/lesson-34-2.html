📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
What can a citizen do between elections?
A republic stays free because of what its citizens do all year, not only on election day. This lesson looks at factions, at the press, at groups that citizens form, and at a plain method for acting on a problem close to home.

Madison on Factions

Elections are not the only way citizens affect government. People also join together to push for what they want. James Madison worried about this in Federalist No. 10. He defined a factionFAK-shunA group of citizens who join together to push their own interest, even when it harms the rights of others or the good of the whole. as a group of citizens, a majority or a minority, who are united by a shared interest that goes against the rights of others or the good of the whole. Madison thought factionsFAK-shunA group of citizens who join together to push their own interest, even when it harms the rights of others or the good of the whole. came naturally, because people differ in wealth, belief, and ambition.

Could the republic get rid of factionsFAK-shunA group of citizens who join together to push their own interest, even when it harms the rights of others or the good of the whole.? Madison said there were only two ways, and both were worse than the problem. One was to destroy liberty, which would be like taking away air because fire uses it. The other was to make everyone think alike, which cannot be done. So he turned to a different goal. He wanted to control the effects of factionFAK-shunA group of citizens who join together to push their own interest, even when it harms the rights of others or the good of the whole.. In a small republic, one factionFAK-shunA group of citizens who join together to push their own interest, even when it harms the rights of others or the good of the whole. can easily become a majority and rule everyone else. In a large republic with many groups, it is hard for any single factionFAK-shunA group of citizens who join together to push their own interest, even when it harms the rights of others or the good of the whole. to gain a majority. And elected representatives, who must answer to many different voters, can refine the public’s wishes.

📚 PART 2 · The Press and the Careful Reader12 min

The Media as Watchdog

The press has another job. Newspapers, television, radio, and websites tell citizens what the government is doing. Reporters watch officials, ask questions, and sometimes uncover wrongdoing that those in power would prefer to hide. You saw this in the Zenger trial, where a printer was tried for criticizing a governor. A free press helps citizens judge their leaders, and it is one reason the First Amendment protects it by name.

But citizens must also read with care. Every message comes from someone, and every writer chooses what to include. BiasBY-usA leaning toward one side that keeps a writer from giving a fair picture. shows up in several ways. A writer may use loaded words, such as calling a plan “a scheme” instead of “a proposal.” A picture or symbol, like a flag or a monster, may stir up feeling without giving any facts. A story may leave out facts that would change a reader’s mind. At its worst, a message becomes propagandaprop-uh-GAN-duhMessages made to change what people believe or do, often by stirring up feeling instead of giving fair facts., made not to inform but to push people toward a belief or an action.

You can use a simple method to judge any source. Ask who made it, who it was made for, and what it wants the audience to do. Then check it against other sources that take different sides. A careful reader does not have to distrust everything. But a careful reader knows that even honest sources have a point of view.

        📚 PART 3 · Acting on a Local Problem13 min

        A Method for Change

        Suppose a dangerous street crossing near your school has no crosswalk. What can a citizen do? You could complain, but complaining is not the same as acting. A better approach has four steps. First, research the problem. Learn how many accidents have happened and what the rules are. Second, find the agency in charge. A street in town belongs to the city or county, not to the Governor or Congress. Third, weigh the alternatives. A crosswalk, a traffic light, a lower speed limit, or a crossing guard might each solve it, and each costs something. Fourth, choose a course of action and carry it out.

        Citizens have many ways to act. They can write to or meet with an official, speak at a public meeting of the city council or school board, sign or start a petitionpuh-TISH-unA written request, usually signed by many people, asking the government to act., join or form a group, support a candidate, or vote. The most effective citizens often combine these. They bring facts, they show that many neighbors agree, and they ask for something specific that the agency has the power to give.

        Notice how this ties the lesson together. The ballot, the press, the interest groupIN-ter-est GROOPA group of people who share a goal and try to influence government to support it., and the petitionpuh-TISH-unA written request, usually signed by many people, asking the government to act. to the city council are the same power used at different scales. A citizen who understands that power can use it close to home, where a single person has the most chance to be heard. That is how a republic stays alive between elections.
