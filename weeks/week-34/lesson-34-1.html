📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
How does a vote become a result, and who decides who wins?
A republic stays free when its people can choose their leaders and accept the outcome. This lesson follows a ballot from the registration desk to the final count, and then looks at the unusual way Americans choose a President.

📚 PART 1 · From Registration to the Count13 min
📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary

A few words worth knowing before you read.

apportionment (uh-POR-shun-ment)
Dividing the seats in the House of Representatives among the states according to population.
elector (ih-LEK-ter)
A person chosen by a state to cast that state’s vote for President.
Electoral College (ih-LEK-ter-ul KOL-ej)
The group of electors from every state who formally choose the President and Vice President.
primary election (PRY-mair-ee ih-LEK-shun)
An election in which members of a party choose the candidate who will run for that party in the general election.
voter registration (VOH-ter rej-ih-STRAY-shun)
Signing up with the government so that your name is on the list of people who may vote.

Who Runs an Election?

Americans vote at three levels. At the local level they choose school board members, mayors, and county officials. At the state level they choose governors and state lawmakers. At the national level they choose members of Congress and the President. The Constitution gives the states the main job of running elections, under rules that Congress may change. That is why voting rules and ballots differ from place to place.

The Constitution also sets age and citizenship rules for national office. A member of the House must be at least twenty-five years old and a citizen for seven years. A senator must be at least thirty and a citizen for nine years. A President must be at least thirty-five, a natural-born citizen, and a resident of the country for fourteen years. These rules are meant to make sure that leaders have some experience and loyalty to the republic.

Follow One Ballot

Now follow a ballot. The first step is voter registrationVOH-ter rej-ih-STRAY-shunSigning up with the government so that your name is on the list of people who may vote., when a citizen signs up so that his or her name is on the voting list. Next come the primary electionsPRY-mair-ee ih-LEK-shunAn election in which members of a party choose the candidate who will run for that party in the general election., in which each party picks its candidates. Then comes the general election, when all voters choose among the candidates. Last is the count, when officials add up the votes and announce the result. Each step has rules, and each can be watched by citizens and by the press. Fair rules matter because a loser can accept a defeat only if he or she trusts the count.

Where power sits in the House depends on a count of a different kind. Every ten years, the census counts the people of the country. The seats in the House are then divided among the states by population, a step called apportionmentuh-POR-shun-mentDividing the seats in the House of Representatives among the states according to population.. Since 1929 the House has been fixed at 435 members. So when people move, as they have from the Northeast toward the South and West, some states gain seats and others lose them. A state that gains seats also gains power in the next presidential election.

📚 PART 2 · The Electoral College and the Election of 180013 min

Why Electors?

Americans do not choose the President by a direct vote. Instead, they vote for electorsih-LEK-terA person chosen by a state to cast that state’s vote for President., who form the Electoral Collegeih-LEK-ter-ul KOL-ejThe group of electors from every state who formally choose the President and Vice President.. Each state has as many electorsih-LEK-terA person chosen by a state to cast that state’s vote for President. as it has members of Congress. The District of Columbia also has three, under the 23rd Amendment. That makes 538 in all, and a candidate needs 270 to win. In nearly every state, the candidate who wins the state’s popular vote wins all of its electorsih-LEK-terA person chosen by a state to cast that state’s vote for President..

Why did the Framers choose this method? They did not want Congress to pick the President, because that would make the President depend on Congress. They also feared that a direct vote of a large and scattered people might be swayed by clever speakers. Alexander Hamilton defended the electorsih-LEK-terA person chosen by a state to cast that state’s vote for President. in Federalist No. 68. He argued that men chosen in each state would judge the candidates calmly. The system also gave the states a role in choosing the President. It has been argued over ever since, because a candidate can win the most votes across the country and still lose in the Electoral Collegeih-LEK-ter-ul KOL-ejThe group of electors from every state who formally choose the President and Vice President..

The Tie of 1800

The election of 1800 showed what could go wrong. In those days each electorih-LEK-terA person chosen by a state to cast that state’s vote for President. cast two votes, with no separate choice for President and Vice President. The candidate with the most votes became President, and the runner-up became Vice President. The Republicans meant for Thomas Jefferson to be President and Aaron Burr to be Vice President. But every Republican electorih-LEK-terA person chosen by a state to cast that state’s vote for President. voted for both men, and they tied with seventy-three votes each.

The Constitution sent the tie to the House of Representatives, where each state had one vote. The House voted thirty-five times without a winner. Hamilton, who disliked Jefferson but distrusted Burr more, urged his Federalist friends to choose Jefferson. On the thirty-sixth ballot, in February 1801, the House elected him. Consider what might have happened if it had failed to choose a President by March 4, when Adams’s term ended. The republic might have had no President at all, and some leaders spoke openly of using force. The 12th Amendment, passed in 1804, fixed the flaw by having electorsih-LEK-terA person chosen by a state to cast that state’s vote for President. cast separate votes for President and Vice President.

📚 PART 3 · Widening the Electorate12 min

More Citizens at the Ballot Box

Who gets to vote has changed a great deal. At the start, most states allowed only white men who owned property to vote. Then came a series of amendments. The 15th, in 1870, said the vote could not be denied because of race. The 19th, in 1920, gave women the vote. The 24th, in 1964, ended the poll tax in federal elections. The 26th, in 1971, lowered the voting age from twenty-one to eighteen. Each time, citizens worked through the amendment process, and the electorate grew.

Notice what stays constant. A free republic depends on elections that are regular, fair, and accepted by the losers. In 1800 the quarrel was bitter, yet the transfer of power from one party to another happened without a war. Citizens need to understand how the process works if they are to protect it. A citizen who knows the steps can register, vote in the primariesPRY-mair-ee ih-LEK-shunAn election in which members of a party choose the candidate who will run for that party in the general election., follow the count, and notice when something goes wrong.
