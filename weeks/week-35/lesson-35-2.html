📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Which kind of economy best serves a free people?
An economy is how a country makes, trades, and shares what people need. Different countries have tried very different systems, and their results can be compared. This lesson sets them side by side.

📚 PART 1 · Three Ways to Run an Economy13 min
📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary

A few words worth knowing before you read.

command economy (kuh-MAND ih-KON-uh-mee)
An economy in which the government decides what to make, how much, and at what price.
communism (KOM-yoo-niz-um)
A system in which the government owns the farms and factories and controls the economy, usually under one ruling party.
GDP per capita (jee-dee-PEE per KAP-ih-tuh)
The value of everything a country produces in a year, divided by the number of people. It is used to compare standards of living.
market economy (MAR-kit ih-KON-uh-mee)
An economy in which private people and businesses decide what to make and sell, and prices are set by buyers and sellers.
mixed economy (MIKST ih-KON-uh-mee)
An economy that is mostly free markets, with some rules and services from the government.

Markets and Commands

Every economy must answer three questions. What should be made? How should it be made? And who gets it? A market economyMAR-kit ih-KON-uh-meeAn economy in which private people and businesses decide what to make and sell, and prices are set by buyers and sellers. lets private people and businesses answer. They own property, compete, and keep profit. Prices rise when something is scarce and drop when it is plentiful, and these signals tell producers what to make. Adam Smith described how this works, and you have studied his idea that people who look after their own interests often serve others by accident.

A command economykuh-MAND ih-KON-uh-meeAn economy in which the government decides what to make, how much, and at what price. answers differently. Government officials decide what farms and factories will make, how much, and at what price. In the most extreme form, called communismKOM-yoo-niz-umA system in which the government owns the farms and factories and controls the economy, usually under one ruling party., the government owns almost everything and a single party rules. Between these two sits the mixed economyMIKST ih-KON-uh-meeAn economy that is mostly free markets, with some rules and services from the government., which includes the United States. Most of the economy is private, but government builds roads and schools, enforces contracts, regulates unfair competition, and collects taxes to pay for public services.

You have also studied how government oversees money. Taxes pay for the services citizens use. Credit lets people borrow, and laws govern lenders. And competition laws, like the Sherman Act, were passed because a market works best when no one can crush all rivals. A free market is not a market without rules. It is a market under law.

📚 PART 2 · What Communism Did13 min

Each system also answers a question about the citizen. In a market economyMAR-kit ih-KON-uh-meeAn economy in which private people and businesses decide what to make and sell, and prices are set by buyers and sellers., a person can own property and earn a living apart from the state. Republicans since Cicero worried that people who depend on a ruler for their bread cannot be truly free, and Jefferson hoped for a country of independent farmers for the same reason. A command economykuh-MAND ih-KON-uh-meeAn economy in which the government decides what to make, how much, and at what price. puts the citizen’s livelihood in the hands of officials.

Effects on Freedom and the Economy

CommunistKOM-yoo-niz-umA system in which the government owns the farms and factories and controls the economy, usually under one ruling party. governments made big promises. They said that if the state took over the economy, everyone would share fairly. But the results were different. In the Soviet Union and in China, there was no free press, no free vote, and no right to start a business or own a farm. Citizens who spoke against the government could be jailed or killed. Because the government set the prices and the goals, it had no honest signals about what people needed, and mistakes piled up.

The worst cost was hunger. In the Soviet Union in the early 1930s, the government forced farmers into state-run farms and took their grain, and millions starved. In China in the late 1950s, a government campaign called the Great Leap Forward led to a famine that killed millions of people. Scholars still argue about the exact numbers, but all agree that they were enormous. These deaths were not caused by bad weather alone. They came from orders that no one could question.

China’s story has a twist. After 1978 its leaders allowed private farming and private business to grow, and the economy grew fast. Hundreds of millions of people climbed out of poverty. But the CommunistKOM-yoo-niz-umA system in which the government owns the farms and factories and controls the economy, usually under one ruling party. Party still rules, with no free press and no free vote. The lesson is that markets raise incomes, but the freedom to speak and vote is a separate matter, and one that markets alone do not guarantee.

Notice also what happened to political voice. In a free republic, citizens can criticize the government, vote it out, and speak up when it makes a mistake. Under communismKOM-yoo-niz-umA system in which the government owns the farms and factories and controls the economy, usually under one ruling party. there was no such check. A leader who made a terrible decision could not be removed, and people who warned of the danger were silenced. Economic control and political control went hand in hand.

📚 PART 3 · Measuring the Results12 min

GDP Per Capita

How can we compare standards of living? One useful number is GDP per capitajee-dee-PEE per KAP-ih-tuhThe value of everything a country produces in a year, divided by the number of people. It is used to compare standards of living.. GDP is the value of everything a country makes in a year. Dividing by the number of people tells us roughly how much that is for each person. The number is not perfect, because it leaves out things like health, freedom, and family. But over many countries it tracks things people care about, such as longer lives and better homes.

History gave us a natural experiment. After the Second World War, Germany was split in two and so was Korea. The people on each side were the same people, with the same language and history. West Germany and South Korea chose markets and elected governments. East Germany and North Korea chose command economieskuh-MAND ih-KON-uh-meeAn economy in which the government decides what to make, how much, and at what price. under one ruling party. Today South Korea’s GDP per capitajee-dee-PEE per KAP-ih-tuhThe value of everything a country produces in a year, divided by the number of people. It is used to compare standards of living. is many times that of North Korea, and when the Berlin Wall fell in 1989, East Germany was far poorer than the West. Same people, different systems, different results.

Why do markets under law do so well? Prices give honest signals. Property gives owners a reason to build and improve. Competition rewards the best ideas and punishes waste. And the rule of law protects contracts and the right to keep what you earn. A market alone cannot do everything. It does not give a poor child a school or stop a polluter, and that is why a republic adds laws and services. But the evidence is clear that economic freedom has raised the standard of living for ordinary people, and political freedom has protected it.

Market economies leave decisions to private people, command economies leave them to the government, and the United States has a mixed economy under the rule of law. Communism took away freedom and brought famine. GDP per capita lets us compare standards of living, and the Korean and German experiments show how much the system matters.
