📚 PART 1 · Florida’s Constitution and the Kinds of Law13 min
📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary

A few words worth knowing before you read.

agency (AY-jen-see)
A part of the government that carries out laws and does a particular job, such as a public works department.
case law (KAYS law)
Law made by judges when they decide cases and explain their reasons.
civil law (SIV-ul law)
Law about disputes between people or groups, usually about money, property, or duties.
criminal law (KRIM-ih-nul law)
Law about crimes. The government charges a person and may punish him or her.
regulation (reg-yuh-LAY-shun)
A rule written by a government agency to carry out a law.
statute (STACH-oot)
A written law passed by a legislature such as Congress or the Florida Legislature.

Florida’s Constitution

Every state has its own constitution. Florida voters approved the one that governs the state today in 1968. It does the same work as the national Constitution. It sets up a legislature, a governor, and courts, and it limits what they can do. But it differs from the national one in some ways. A state constitution may not contradict the national Constitution. When the two clash, the national one wins.

First, it begins with a Declaration of Rights. Article I lists the rights of the people before it says anything about government. The United States Constitution put its list in amendments, after the rest. Putting rights first says that they come before the government does.

Second, Florida’s constitution is much longer. State governments handle more of daily life, and so they need more rules. Third, it is easier to change. Citizens can sometimes put an amendment on the ballot themselves, and most changes need at least sixty percent of the voters. The Legislature and special commissions can also put amendments on the ballot. The national Constitution has been amended only twenty-seven times.

Criminal and Civil

Law is also sorted into two large kinds. Criminal lawKRIM-ih-nul lawLaw about crimes. The government charges a person and may punish him or her. deals with crimes, such as theft or assault. The government brings the charge, and the punishment may be a fine or jail. Civil lawSIV-ul lawLaw about disputes between people or groups, usually about money, property, or duties. deals with disputes between people or groups. Two neighbors may argue over a fence, or a customer may sue a business over a broken contract. The judge decides who must pay or who must act. You can meet both kinds of law in state courts. One event can lead to both. A driver who causes a crash may be charged with a crime and sued for the damage.

📚 PART 2 · A Citizen at Work13 min

Who Provides What

Each level of government provides services. Local governments provide police, fire protection, schools, and water. State governments build roads, run universities, and operate courts. The national government handles defense, the mail, and Social Security. Next you will see how each level pays for what it does. Knowing who provides what is the first thing a citizen needs. A person who calls the President about a pothole will wait a long time.

🏛️ Cities: Tallahassee

Florida’s capital was chosen in 1824. Tallahassee sat about halfway between St. Augustine in the east and Pensacola in the west, the two oldest Florida towns. A site in the middle meant that lawmakers from both sides had about the same distance to travel.

Four Steps for a Local Problem

Most citizens will never write a law. But any citizen can work on a local problem. Here is a method with four steps. First, research the problem. Find out what is wrong, where, and for how long. Second, find the right agencyAY-jen-seeA part of the government that carries out laws and does a particular job, such as a public works department.. Third, think about alternatives and weigh what each would cost and who would gain. Local agenciesAY-jen-seeA part of the government that carries out laws and does a particular job, such as a public works department. include the city council, the county commission, and the school board, and each has its own job. Fourth, choose a course of action and carry it out.

Here is a model. In parts of Miami Beach, streets flood during very high tides. A citizen would begin by learning when and where the water rises. The next step is to find the right agencyAY-jen-seeA part of the government that carries out laws and does a particular job, such as a public works department., which is likely the city’s public works department and the city commission that oversees it. The citizen would learn that the city has installed pumps and raised some roads.

Then comes the weighing. Pumps cost money, and so does raising a road. Who should pay, the homeowners on that street or the whole city? Each choice helps some people more than others. The citizen decides what to support and takes action by writing to officials, speaking at a public meeting, or joining a group of neighbors. A small group that attends a meeting often matters more than a large group that stays home. Officials hear most from the people who show up.

Notice that this problem touches the whole lesson. The city runs the pumps, the state sets many of the rules, and the nation may offer money. A citizen who knows which level has the power can take the problem to the right door. Notice, too, that the same method works for a crowded school, a broken streetlight, or a dangerous crossing. The steps stay the same. Only the agencyAY-jen-seeA part of the government that carries out laws and does a particular job, such as a public works department. and the choices change. Learn the method once, and you can use it for the rest of your life.
