📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Why would a republic divide its power between a nation and its states?
Most citizens meet their government close to home. This lesson asks why the Framers kept strong states inside a national union, and how North America is divided.

Power Divided

Under federalism, power is divided between a national government and state governments. Each is a real government, with its own laws, courts, and officers. The people live under both at once. You have studied this idea before. Here we look at it from the point of view of a citizen who lives in one state and travels across many.

Article IV of the Constitution deals with how the states relate to each other. It has four main rules. The first is full faith and creditful fayth and KRED-itThe rule that each state must respect the laws, records, and court decisions of the other states.. A court judgment or a public record from one state must be respected in the others. If you win a lawsuit in Georgia, you can collect in Florida. The second is privileges and immunitiesPRIV-ih-lij-ez and ih-MYOO-nih-teezThe rule that a state must treat citizens of other states fairly, as it treats its own.. A state cannot treat citizens of other states unfairly, so a person from Ohio may travel to Florida, own property, and use its courts.

The third rule lets Congress admit new states. Florida became the twenty-seventh state in 1845 by this rule. The fourth is a promise that the nation will guarantee each state a republican form of government. This is the republic’s protection of itself. The national government also stands ready to help a state against invasion. No state may turn into a kingdom while it sits inside the union.

Few and Defined, Numerous and Indefinite

The 10th Amendment states the main rule. Powers that the Constitution does not give to the national government, and does not forbid to the states, belong to the states or to the people. James Madison explained this in Federalist No. 45. The powers of the national government, he said, are “few and defined,” and those of the states are “numerous and indefinite.”

Madison meant that the nation handles a short list of large tasks, such as defense, foreign affairs, and money. The states handle most of daily life, such as schools, roads, local crime, and family law. Many of the laws that touch you most are state or local laws.

Why Divide Power?

The Framers had two main reasons. The first was self-government. People are more likely to take part in a government that is close to them. A town or a state can decide things that matter to its own people. A state can also try a new idea. If the idea fails, the harm stays small, and the rest of the country learns from it. The second was safety. Montesquieu had argued that power in one set of hands is dangerous. Dividing power between the nation and the states adds another check on it. If one level of government goes wrong, the other may stand in the way.

📚 PART 2 · Lines on the Map12 min

Borders and Divisions

Geography helps us see federalism. The United States shares its northern border with Canada. It is one of the longest borders between two countries in the world. To the south, the Rio Grande forms much of the border with Mexico. These lines were set by treaties and wars. The Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, which ended the war with Mexico in 1848, moved the border to the Rio Grande and far to the west. Borders affect trade, travel, and defense.

Each of North America’s three large countries is divided inside. The United States has fifty states, along with territories such as Puerto Rico. Canada has ten provincesPROV-insA main division of some countries, such as Canada, like a state in the United States. and three territories. Mexico has thirty-one states and its capital, Mexico City. A state and a provincePROV-insA main division of some countries, such as Canada, like a state in the United States. look alike on a map, but each fits into its own country’s system of government.

Regions Made by Land and Settlement

Within the United States, people often speak of cultural regionsKUL-chur-ul REE-junAn area whose people share a way of life, shaped by land, climate, and history.. Land, rivers, climate, and the people who settled each place shaped them. The Northeast has harbors and small farms, and it became an early center of trade and factories. The South has warm weather and long river valleys. Its economy grew on farming, and before the Civil War much of that farming depended on enslaved labor. Florida belongs to the South.

The Midwest has flat, rich farmland, the Great Lakes, and the Mississippi. Canals and railroads tied it to the East. The Southwest is dry land that was Spanish and then Mexican before it was American, and its ranching and its Native American and Hispanic traditions are still strong. The West has mountains, big distances, and a Pacific coast. These regions are not walls. People move, trade, and marry across them, and many places belong to two regions at once.

These regions help explain why federalism fits America. Places with different land, work, and history will often want different laws. A rule that suits a farming state may not suit a harbor city. Federalism lets each state solve its own problems, while the nation holds them together.

It also creates tension. When should the nation insist on one rule for everyone, and when should it leave the choice to a state? That is a question you will argue about in the discussion, and it has been argued since the first Congress met.
