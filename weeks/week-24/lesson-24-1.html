📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
How can a government borrow money and still be trusted?
The new republic had won its war, but it owed a great deal of money. This lesson asks why credit matters, how Alexander Hamilton planned to fix the nation’s debts, and why Thomas Jefferson feared the bank that came with the plan.

Borrowing and Trust

Think about what it means to borrow. A lender hands over money today. The borrower promises to give it back later, and to pay interestIN-trestThe fee a borrower pays for the use of borrowed money, usually a percentage of the amount. as the fee for using it. CreditKRED-itThe ability to borrow money now and promise to pay it back later. is built on trust. A lender will not hand over money to a borrower who has a record of not paying it back.

CreditKRED-itThe ability to borrow money now and promise to pay it back later. has real advantages. A family can buy a house and pay for it over many years. A farmer can borrow to buy seed and repay after the harvest. A business can borrow to build a factory that will earn the money back. CreditKRED-itThe ability to borrow money now and promise to pay it back later. has costs too. A borrower who cannot repay may lose the house, the farm, or the business. Every loan carries a risk, and the risk lands on both sides. The lender may lose the money, and the borrower may lose far more than that.

A Republic in Debt

In 1789 the new republic had bad creditKRED-itThe ability to borrow money now and promise to pay it back later.. The Revolution had been paid for with borrowed money. The national government owed about fifty million dollars, some of it to France and the Netherlands and most of it to its own citizens, such as soldiers and farmers who had been paid in paper promises. The states owed another twenty-five million or so. The old Congress had no power to tax, and it had paid little. Many of those promises were selling for a small part of their face value.

George Washington asked his Secretary of the Treasury, Alexander Hamilton, for a plan. In January 1790 Hamilton sent Congress his Report on Public CreditKRED-itThe ability to borrow money now and promise to pay it back later.. His argument was simple. A nation that keeps its word will be trusted, and a nation that is trusted can borrow when it must. He asked Congress to pay the national debtDETMoney that a person, business, or government owes. in full. He also asked the national government to assumeuh-SUMP-shunHere, the national government taking over the debts of the states and promising to pay them. the debtsDETMoney that a person, business, or government owes. of the states.

Not everyone liked this. Some states, such as Virginia, had already paid most of their debtsDETMoney that a person, business, or government owes. and did not want to pay again through national taxes. Some people also objected that speculators had bought up old promises from poor soldiers at low prices and would now be paid in full. Was that fair? You will weigh that question in the discussion.

📚 PART 2 · A Bargain and a Bank13 min

The Bargain of 1790

In the summer of 1790 Hamilton’s plan was stuck in Congress. At the same time, Northerners and Southerners were arguing over where to put the nation’s permanent capital. Jefferson later wrote that he brought Hamilton and James Madison together over dinner. The three men reached a bargain. Madison and other Virginians would let the assumptionuh-SUMP-shunHere, the national government taking over the debts of the states and promising to pay them. of state debtsDETMoney that a person, business, or government owes. pass. In return, the capital would be built on the Potomac River, on the border between the North and the South. Congress passed both measures that summer.

It was a close call. Without the bargain, the plan might have failed, and the republic’s creditKRED-itThe ability to borrow money now and promise to pay it back later. would have stayed weak. Politics, as often happens, was a matter of trading one thing for another. The nation’s capital, Washington, D.C., came out of that bargain.

The First Bank of the United States

Hamilton wanted more. In 1791 he asked Congress to charterCHAR-terA written grant of power from a government that lets a bank or company exist and do business. a national bank. The Bank of the United States would hold the government’s money, make loans, and issue notes that people could trust. It would work as an early central bankSEN-trul BANKA bank for the nation as a whole that helps manage the supply of money and steadies other banks., helping to steady the nation’s money. Congress agreed, and Washington signed the bill after hearing both sides.

Jefferson objected on two grounds. The first was the Constitution. Nowhere does it say that Congress may charterCHAR-terA written grant of power from a government that lets a bank or company exist and do business. a bank, he argued, and so Congress may not do it. Hamilton answered that the Constitution lets Congress make all laws that are “necessary and proper” for carrying out its listed powers, such as collecting taxes and borrowing money. That was the first great argument about how broadly to read the Constitution. You will see it again when the Supreme Court decides McCulloch v. Maryland later in the course.

Jefferson’s second ground was the old fear of corruption. Classical writers had warned that a republic is in danger when the rich grow powerful and the government becomes dependent on them. Jefferson saw a class of bankers and bondholders tied to the government by money. He wanted a republic of independent farmers who owed no one. Hamilton thought a modern nation had to have creditKRED-itThe ability to borrow money now and promise to pay it back later. and banks to grow strong. Both men loved the republic. They disagreed about what it needed.

Watching the Lenders Today

The First Bank’s charterCHAR-terA written grant of power from a government that lets a bank or company exist and do business. ran out in 1811, and the country later tried a second bank. Banking troubles came again and again. In 1913 Congress created the Federal Reserve System, which serves as the central bankSEN-trul BANKA bank for the nation as a whole that helps manage the supply of money and steadies other banks. today. It helps steady the supply of money and acts to keep banks sound.

During the Great Depression, thousands of banks failed, and families lost their savings. In 1933 Congress created the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation. It is an agencyAY-jen-seeA part of the government that carries out laws and does a particular job. that insures the money you put in a bank, up to a set amount. AgenciesAY-jen-seeA part of the government that carries out laws and does a particular job. also write regulationsreg-yuh-LAY-shunA rule written by a government agency to carry out a law. for banks, and they check that banks follow them. A republic that wants creditKRED-itThe ability to borrow money now and promise to pay it back later. to stay trustworthy has learned that someone must watch the lenders.
