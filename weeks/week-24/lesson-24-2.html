Paying for the Debt

Hamilton’s plan promised to pay the nation’s debts. To do that, the government needed steady income. Most of it came from taxes on goods that came in through the ports. But more was needed, and in 1791 Congress passed an excise taxEK-syze taxA tax on a particular product, such as whiskey, gasoline, or tobacco. on whiskey made in the United States.

To easterners the tax looked sensible. It fell on a product that people could do without, and the money would help pay the debt. To farmers on the western frontier it looked very different. Think about the land. Western Pennsylvania lay across the Appalachian Mountains, far from eastern cities. There were few good roads, and moving heavy goods over the mountains was slow and costly. A farmer with a wagonload of grain could earn little at market once the cost of the trip was paid.

Whiskey solved the problem. A farmer could turn bulky grain into whiskey, which was worth much more for its weight. A pack horse could carry a load of whiskey that held the value of many times as much grain. Whiskey was also used almost like money in places where coins were scarce. So the excise taxEK-syze taxA tax on a particular product, such as whiskey, gasoline, or tobacco. was not a tax on a luxury. It fell on the one product that let a frontier farmer reach a market. Many of them felt that the new government was taxing them for being far away.

They also remembered the Revolution. Only twenty years before, Americans had protested taxes passed by a government they did not control. Now some westerners said that a distant Congress was doing the same thing, and they met, signed petitions, and refused to pay. Tax collectors were threatened, and some were beaten.

📚 PART 2 · Washington Chooses12 min

The Whiskey Rebellion, 1794

By the summer of 1794 the protest had grown into an insurrectionin-sur-EK-shunA violent uprising against a government.. In July, a crowd attacked the home of a tax inspector near Pittsburgh. Thousands of armed men gathered, and talk of breaking away from the nation was heard in the West.

President Washington had to decide. If a law could be defied by an armed crowd, the national government would not be a government at all. This was the first big test of whether the new Constitution could enforce its own laws against citizens. Under the Constitution, he could call out the state militias to put down an insurrectionin-sur-EK-shunA violent uprising against a government..

Washington chose force first, and mercy after. In August 1794 he issued a proclamation ordering the rebels to go home. At the same time, he sent commissioners to talk with them, and he called up a militiamuh-LISH-uhA force of citizens, not full-time soldiers, who are called up to serve in an emergency. army of about thirteen thousand men from Pennsylvania, New Jersey, Maryland, and Virginia. Washington himself rode with the troops for part of the way, and Hamilton went with them. When the army marched west in the autumn, the rebels melted away. Only a few were arrested. Two men were convicted of treason, and Washington pardonedPAR-dunAn official act of forgiveness that frees a person from punishment for a crime. both of them.

The outcome was not certain. Washington might have waited and let the protest burn itself out, and the national government would have looked weak. He might have punished hard, and he would have made martyrs of farmers. He did neither. He showed that the republic would enforce its laws and also that it could be merciful. The tax was repealed in 1802, after Jefferson became President.

The rebellion taught lessons to both sides. The farmers learned that the new government was not a distant king and that it would act. They also learned that they had a better road open to them. In a republic, citizens who dislike a law can petition Congress, write to newspapers, and vote against the lawmakers who passed it. Many western farmers did just that, and they helped to elect Jefferson. The government learned that it could keep order without bloodshed, and that a show of strength, followed by forgiveness, can win back citizens who were angry.

📚 PART 3 · Taxes and Services Today12 min

Who Taxes What

Every level of government raises money. At the national level, the largest sources are the income taxIN-kum taxA tax on the money a person or business earns. and the payroll taxPAY-rohl taxA tax taken from paychecks to pay for Social Security and Medicare.. The income taxIN-kum taxA tax on the money a person or business earns. comes out of what people and businesses earn. The payroll taxPAY-rohl taxA tax taken from paychecks to pay for Social Security and Medicare. comes out of paychecks and pays for Social Security and Medicare. States rely heavily on sales taxes, which are added to the price of what you buy. Florida has no income taxIN-kum taxA tax on the money a person or business earns. on people at all. Local governments depend mostly on property taxesPROP-er-tee taxA yearly tax on land and buildings, used mostly by local governments., which are paid on land and buildings and used for schools, police, and fire protection.

The national income taxIN-kum taxA tax on the money a person or business earns. was not always possible. The Constitution limited direct taxes, and the Supreme Court struck down an income taxIN-kum taxA tax on the money a person or business earns. in the 1890s. In 1913 the states ratified the 16th Amendment, which lets Congress tax incomes. Over the next decades, that tax grew into the main source of money for the national government, and it helped pay for defense, roads, and programs for older Americans.

You meet all of these taxes in a normal week. Suppose you buy a pair of shoes. Part of the price is sales tax, which goes to the state and the county. A parent’s paycheck shows money taken out for income taxIN-kum taxA tax on the money a person or business earns. and payroll taxPAY-rohl taxA tax taken from paychecks to pay for Social Security and Medicare. before it ever reaches the bank. Your family’s home is taxed by the county each year, and that money helps pay for your school. Each level of government takes a share, and each spends its share on a different set of jobs. Knowing which tax pays for which service helps a citizen ask good questions at a public meeting.

What a Government Owes Its People

Citizens do not only pay. They expect things in return. The Preamble says the Constitution was written to “promote the general welfare.” That is why governments run schools, build roads, protect the public, and keep courts open. Citizens may disagree about how much a government should do, and how much it should tax to do it. That argument is part of self-government. But the argument starts from the same fact Washington faced. A government that cannot raise money cannot keep its promises, and a government that takes too much from its citizens loses their trust.
