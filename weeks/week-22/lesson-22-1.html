New York, 1733

In 1733 New York was a busy port town of about ten thousand people. The king had sent a new governor, William Cosby. Many colonists thought he was greedy and unfair. He had even removed the colony’s chief justice after the judge ruled against him. The governor’s opponents had no newspaper of their own, so they backed a new one, the New-York Weekly Journal.

The printer was John Peter Zenger, an immigrant from Germany. His paper mocked the governor and accused him of abusing his power. Cosby was furious. In November 1734 he had Zenger arrested and charged with seditious libelsih-DISH-us LY-bulUnder old law, the crime of printing words that criticized the government or its officials.. Bail was set so high that he could not pay it, and Zenger sat in jail for months. His wife, Anna, kept the paper going by passing messages through the door of his cell and printing each week’s issue.

The law of that time was harsh. A libelLY-bulA false or harmful statement, written or printed, that hurts a person’s good name. was a false or harmful statement printed about a person. Seditious libelsih-DISH-us LY-bulUnder old law, the crime of printing words that criticized the government or its officials. was worse. It meant printing anything that made people think badly of the government. Under the old rule, it did not matter if the words were true. Lawyers had a saying: the greater the truth, the greater the libelLY-bulA false or harmful statement, written or printed, that hurts a person’s good name.. Truth only made the insult sharper.

The judges had another power. In a libelLY-bulA false or harmful statement, written or printed, that hurts a person’s good name. case, the judge decided whether the words were libelLY-bulA false or harmful statement, written or printed, that hurts a person’s good name.. The jury was only asked one thing: did the defendant print them? Zenger had printed them, and everyone knew it. By the rules of the court, the trial should have been over before it began.

Andrew Hamilton Speaks

Zenger’s friends needed a brave lawyer. The judges had thrown his first two lawyers out of the case. Then, in August 1735, a famous Philadelphia lawyer named Andrew Hamilton came to New York. He was an old man, and he was not the same person as Alexander Hamilton. He took the case.

Hamilton did something bold. He admitted that Zenger had printed the words. Then he argued that the words were true, and that a person should not be punished for printing the truth about a ruler. He turned to the jurors. He said that the case was not only about one poor printer. According to the printed account of the trial, he called it “the cause of liberty.”

The judge told the jurors to obey the rule. LibelLY-bulA false or harmful statement, written or printed, that hurts a person’s good name. was a question for the court, he said, and they should find only that Zenger had printed the words. But the jurors listened to Hamilton. After a short time, they came back and said “not guilty.” The courtroom cheered.

It Might Have Gone the Other Way

This was a real turning point. The jury could have done what the judge told them. Most juries of that time did. If they had, Zenger would have been punished, and other printers would have learned to keep quiet. Instead twelve ordinary men said no to a governor and a judge. They acquitteduh-KWITTo decide that a person charged with a crime is not guilty. Zenger. A jury is made of neighbors, and neighbors knew what it was like to live under a governor like Cosby.

Why does a free press matter so much? Citizens cannot judge their rulers if they cannot learn what the rulers are doing. A paper that may print only praise is not a window. It is a mirror held up by the government.

The verdict did not change the law that day. Judges still held on to many of their old powers. But newspapers saw that a jury might protect them, and later laws made truth a defense in a libelLY-bulA false or harmful statement, written or printed, that hurts a person’s good name. case. People also began to see that they have a right to speak about their rulers and a way to protect that right.

The Steps of a Trial Then and Now

A criminal trial today has a clear order. First, the government charges a person with a crime. In serious cases, a grand jurygrand JOOR-eeA group of citizens that decides whether there is enough evidence to bring a person to trial. hears the evidence and decides if there is enough to go to trial. Then a second jury, called the trial jury, listens to both sides. The trial jury decides whether the person is guilty.

The government has the burden of proofBUR-dun uv proofThe duty to prove a charge. In a criminal trial, the government must carry it.. The accused does not have to prove that he is innocent. The government must prove its case beyond a reasonable doubt. In a criminal trial the verdict of the jury must be unanimousyoo-NAN-ih-musAgreed to by everyone, with no one against.. One juror who is not convinced can stop a conviction.

There are state courts and federal courts. Most crimes are tried in state courts, because they are crimes under state law. Federal courts hear crimes against federal law. A jury works the same way in both.

The Jury as a Check

Earlier in this course you studied checks and balances. The jury is one of them. It is made of citizens, not officers of the government. When a prosecutor wants to use the power of the state against a person, ordinary neighbors have to agree first. A jury can refuse to convict when the government’s case is weak or unjust.

That is why the jury is more than a duty. It is a share in ruling, as you learned. Zenger was saved by twelve people who were not experts, who had no reason to please the governor, and who trusted their own sense of right and wrong.
