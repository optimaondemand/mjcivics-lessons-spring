📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary

A few words worth knowing before you read.

bias (BY-us)
A leaning toward one side that keeps a person or a source from being fair.
interest group (IN-trist groop)
A group of citizens who join together to influence government on an issue they care about.
pardon (PAR-dun)
An official act that forgives a person for a crime and ends the punishment.
propaganda (prop-uh-GAN-duh)
Information, often one-sided or misleading, that is spread to push people toward a belief or an action.
sedition (sih-DISH-un)
Speech or writing that urges people to turn against the government.

A War Scare

The nation had learned about Zenger, but it did not always remember. In 1798 the United States was in an undeclared naval war with France. French ships were seizing American vessels. Many Americans feared spies and troublemakers at home. Congress passed a law that let the President deport foreigners he thought were dangerous. President John Adams and a Congress controlled by Federalists passed a set of new laws. One of them was the Seditionsih-DISH-unSpeech or writing that urges people to turn against the government. Act.

The Act made it a crime to print “false, scandalous and malicious” writing against the government, Congress, or the President. The penalty was a fine and up to two years in prison. The law did not stop with the old rule. It said that truth could be a defense. Yet it left out the Vice President, who then was Thomas Jefferson, the leader of the other party.

Supporters said that a country at war could not allow lies that weaken it. The trouble was how the law was used. Almost every person tried was a Republican editor or writer who criticized the Federalists. Matthew Lyon, a congressman from Vermont, was jailed for four months for printing criticism of President Adams. Voters in his state reelected him while he sat in jail.

Jefferson and James Madison answered with the Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions. They said that the Seditionsih-DISH-unSpeech or writing that urges people to turn against the government. Act broke the First Amendment, and that states could speak out against it. The law ended in March 1801, and President Jefferson pardonedPAR-dunAn official act that forgives a person for a crime and ends the punishment. the people who had been convicted. A republic that had defended a printer in 1735 had come close to silencing printers in 1798.

Think about the two sides. Congress felt it was protecting a young country. Critics felt it was using the law to punish its enemies. This is a hard question, and you will argue about it in the discussion that follows. The Act did allow truth as a defense, which Zenger’s lawyer had asked for. Yet it was used mostly against one party’s writers, and it left the Vice President unprotected. Ask yourself whether a law can be fair on paper and unfair in use.

      How Citizens Organize

      A citizen alone has a small voice. Citizens who join together have a bigger one. An interest groupIN-trist groopA group of citizens who join together to influence government on an issue they care about. is a group of people who unite to influence government on a cause they share. Madison saw this in Federalist No. 10. He called such groups factions, and he feared they could hurt the common good. He also knew that you cannot ban them without banning freedom itself.

      Alexis de Tocqueville, a Frenchman who visited America in the 1830s, noticed how often Americans formed groups. They started clubs, churches, and societies to solve problems themselves. He thought this habit helped keep the republic free. Today interest groupsIN-trist groopA group of citizens who join together to influence government on an issue they care about. write letters, hold marches, and speak to lawmakers. Used well, they help citizens take part. The First Amendment protects the right to assemble and to petition the government, and that is the right they use.

      The news media do a related job. They watch the government and tell citizens what they find. Ordinary people do this too when they write a letter to a local paper or call an official. Each of these is a way to monitor those in power and to try to influence them.

      Check in
      Think of a group you or someone you know has joined to change something, like a club, a team, or a neighborhood effort. What did joining together make possible? There is no wrong answer.

      Reading With Care

      Citizens also need to judge what they read. Every source has a point of view. A source may show biasBY-usA leaning toward one side that keeps a person or a source from being fair. in its loaded words, which are words that push a feeling. It may use symbols, such as flags or pictures, to stir pride or fear. It may leave out facts that would change your mind. Omission can be the quietest kind of biasBY-usA leaning toward one side that keeps a person or a source from being fair.. Even a single word can lean. Two writers describe the same crowd in the street. One calls it a gathering, and the other calls it a mob. The facts are the same, but the feeling is not.

      The worst case is propagandaprop-uh-GAN-duhInformation, often one-sided or misleading, that is spread to push people toward a belief or an action., which is information spread to push people toward a belief or an action. It is often one-sided or misleading. Two famous examples come from American history. In the 1890s, two rival New York newspapers fought for readers with huge headlines and dramatic stories. This was called the yellow press. After the battleship Maine exploded in 1898, many papers blamed Spain with little proof, and the public demanded war.

      In 1917 the government itself did something similar. President Wilson created the Committee on Public Information. It put out posters, films, and speeches to build support for the war. Many of them told the truth, and many played on fear. A citizen needs to know the difference. A poster that shows a smiling soldier, a waving flag, and the words “Do your part” is built to make you feel something before you think. That is not always wrong. But it is always worth noticing.

      Three Questions

      Here is a method for judging any source. Ask who made it. Ask who it was made for. Ask what it wants you to do. If you can answer all three, you are less likely to be fooled. You will use these questions again when you study a republic that was undone by propagandaprop-uh-GAN-duhInformation, often one-sided or misleading, that is spread to push people toward a belief or an action..

      Apply the method
      Use the lesson’s examples to practice judging a source.

      In 1917 the Committee on Public Information put out posters, films, and speeches. Which question does that fact answer?
