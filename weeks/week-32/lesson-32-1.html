📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Who holds power in the United States: the nation, the states, or both?
The Framers answered the failure of the Articles and the fear of a single ruler at the same moment. They divided power between a national government and the states. This lesson follows that line and a famous case about where it falls.

📚 PART 1 · Three Kinds of Power13 min
📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary

A few words worth knowing before you read.

concurrent powers (kun-KUR-ent POW-erz)
Powers shared by the national government and the states.
delegated powers (DEL-ih-gay-ted POW-erz)
Powers the Constitution gives to the national government.
implied powers (im-PLYD POW-erz)
Powers not listed in the Constitution but reasonably needed to carry out the listed ones.
reserved powers (rih-ZURVD POW-erz)
Powers kept by the states or the people. The Tenth Amendment protects them.
unitary system (YOO-nih-tair-ee SIS-tum)
A system in which one central government holds the power, and local governments have only what it gives them.

Dividing Power by Level

You have studied how the Constitution divides power among branches. It also divides power by level. The Framers wanted a national government strong enough to defend the country and to keep trade open between states. They also wanted the states to keep their own governments, close to the people. The result is a system called federalism, in which power is shared between one national government and many state governments.

Think of three kinds of power. Delegated powersDEL-ih-gay-ted POW-erzPowers the Constitution gives to the national government. belong to the national government. These are the powers listed in the Constitution, such as coining money, declaring war, making treaties, and regulating trade between the states. Reserved powersrih-ZURVD POW-erzPowers kept by the states or the people. The Tenth Amendment protects them. belong to the states. The Tenth Amendment says that powers the Constitution does not give to the nation, and does not forbid to the states, are reservedrih-ZURVD POW-erzPowers kept by the states or the people. The Tenth Amendment protects them. to the states or to the people. States run most schools and local police, and they make most laws about marriage, property, and ordinary crimes. Concurrent powerskun-KUR-ent POW-erzPowers shared by the national government and the states. are shared. Both the nation and the states may tax, build roads, and set up courts.

Other parts of the Constitution fill out the picture. Article IV says that every state must respect the laws and court rulings of the others, that a citizen of one state has the same basic privileges in any other, and that the nation guarantees every state a republican form of government. And the Supremacy Clause in Article VI says that when the Constitution, a national law, or a treaty conflicts with a state law, the national law wins. Without this clause, federalism would slide back into the confusion of the Articles.

        📚 PART 2 · McCulloch v. Maryland13 min

        A Bank and a Tax

        Where exactly is the line? In 1819 the Supreme Court had to say. In 1816 Congress had created the Second Bank of the United States, with branches in several states. Many people disliked it. Jefferson had once argued that the Constitution did not give Congress the power to charter a bank. Maryland tried to push the Bank out by placing a heavy tax on its branch in Baltimore. A bank officer named James McCulloch refused to pay.

        The case raised two questions. First, does Congress have the power to create a bank, since the word “bank” appears nowhere in the Constitution? Second, can a state tax an agency of the national government? Chief Justice John Marshall answered both for a unanimous Court.

        On the first, Marshall said yes. Congress has powers over taxes, borrowing, and trade, and a bank helps carry them out. Under the Necessary and Proper Clause, Congress may choose the means that are appropriate to a legitimate end. These are the implied powersim-PLYD POW-erzPowers not listed in the Constitution but reasonably needed to carry out the listed ones.. They are not listed, but they follow from the ones that are. On the second question, Marshall said no. A state could not tax an agency of the nation. “The power to tax involves the power to destroy,” he wrote, and if states could tax national agencies, they could destroy the national government itself.

        Think about what might have happened if the Court had gone the other way. If Marshall had ruled for Maryland, Congress would have been confined to the powers listed in the Constitution and nothing more. States could have taxed the national government at will, and the Union might have gone back toward the weakness of the Articles. The decision made the national government stronger, but it was reasoned from the Constitution’s own words, and the debate over how far implied powersim-PLYD POW-erzPowers not listed in the Constitution but reasonably needed to carry out the listed ones. go has never ended.

        📚 PART 3 · Why Federalism? And Other Systems12 min

        The Advantages

        Why divide power between levels? There are two main reasons. The first is that federalism lets people govern themselves close to home while still belonging to a large and strong nation. A town can decide about its own schools and streets, and the nation can protect all the states from foreign enemies. The second is that federalism guards against tyranny. If power is split between the nation and fifty states, any one government that begins to grab too much faces others who can resist. Citizens can also compare the states, and move to one with laws they prefer.

        Check in
        Federalism has two main advantages: people govern themselves close to home, and power split between levels guards against tyranny. Which matters more to you? There is no wrong answer.

        Three Systems Compared

        Governments around the world divide power in three main ways. In a federal system, such as the United States, power is shared between a national government and state governments, and each has its own sphere written into a constitution. In a unitary systemYOO-nih-tair-ee SIS-tumA system in which one central government holds the power, and local governments have only what it gives them., one central government holds the power. Local governments exist, but only because the center allows them. France is an example. In a confederal system, the states hold most of the power, and the central government can do only what the states allow. The Articles of Confederation were a confederal system, and you have seen how it failed.

        The American system tries to avoid both the weakness of a confederation and the danger of a government with no rival. It gives the nation real power and gives the states real power too. The courts decide the quarrels between them, as in McCulloch. The question of where state power ends is not settled once and for all. Every generation of citizens, courts, and Congresses returns to it.
