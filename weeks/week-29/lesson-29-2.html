📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Why did colonists who loved the British Crown decide to leave it?
For more than a hundred years, the colonists governed themselves in many ways. Then Britain tried to change that. This lesson follows the policies one by one and asks which right each one touched.

2

3
I will be able to match grievances in the Declaration to the natural rights it says were violated

The Bill for a War

In 1763 Britain won the French and Indian War and took control of a huge stretch of North America. But the war had nearly doubled the nation’s debt. Parliament decided that the colonists, who had been defended, should help pay. At the same time, the king issued the Proclamation of 1763. It forbade colonists to settle west of the Appalachian Mountains, in order to keep the peace with Native nations and avoid new wars. But many colonists had fought for that land, and they felt cheated. Mountains and distance were now causing a quarrel.

Taxes and Protest

Parliament then passed laws to raise money. The Sugar Act of 1764 put dutiesDOO-teezA tax placed on goods that are brought in from other places. on goods brought into the colonies. The Stamp Act of 1765 taxed printed papers of every kind, such as newspapers, contracts, and even playing cards. It was the first tax placed directly on the colonists. They answered that only their own elected assemblies could tax them, since they had no members in Parliament. “No taxation without representation” became their cry. Merchants boycottedBOY-kotTo refuse to buy or use something as a way of protesting. British goods, and crowds in the streets frightened the tax collectors.

The protest worked. In 1766 Parliament repealed the Stamp Act. But on the same day it passed the Declaratory Act, which said that Parliament could make laws for the colonies in all cases. Peace had returned, but the question underneath had not been answered. Who had the right to tax and govern the colonists?

In 1767 the Townshend Acts put new dutiesDOO-teezA tax placed on goods that are brought in from other places. on glass, paper, paint, and tea. Colonists boycottedBOY-kotTo refuse to buy or use something as a way of protesting. again. Britain sent soldiers to Boston to keep order. On March 5, 1770, a crowd taunted a group of soldiers, and the soldiers fired. Five colonists were killed in what was called the Boston Massacre. John Adams, a Boston lawyer who was no friend of mobs, defended the soldiers in court, and most were found not guilty. He believed that every man deserves a fair trial, even in an unpopular case.

📚 PART 2 · The Last Roads to Peace13 min

Tea and Punishment

In 1773 Parliament passed the Tea Act to help a struggling British company. The colonists saw it as another way to force a tax on them. In December, a group of men in Boston dumped hundreds of chests of tea into the harbor. Parliament answered in 1774 with laws the colonists called the Intolerable Acts. They closed Boston’s port until the tea was paid for, took away much of Massachusetts’s self-government, and again allowed the quarteringKWOR-ter-ingRequiring people to give soldiers a place to stay in their homes. of soldiers. Other colonies now saw that what had happened to Massachusetts could happen to them. They sent delegates to the First Continental Congress.

Shots and a Plea

On April 19, 1775, British soldiers marched to Concord, Massachusetts, to seize military supplies. At Lexington and Concord, they met armed colonists, and the first shots of the war were fired. Even then, many delegates in the Second Continental Congress hoped that the quarrel might be settled. In July 1775 they sent the Olive Branch Petitionpeh-TISH-unA formal request, usually in writing, sent to a government or ruler. to King George III. The petitionpeh-TISH-unA formal request, usually in writing, sent to a government or ruler. declared loyalty to the Crown and begged him to stop the fighting and to settle the dispute.

The king refused even to read it. Instead he declared that the colonies were in rebellion. Here was a road not taken. A king who listened might have kept the colonies. Reconciliation was still open until he closed it. Only after that did many colonists, such as those who read Paine’s Common Sense the next January, decide that independence was the only road left.

📚 PART 3 · Matching Grievances to Rights12 min

The Declaration as a Charge Sheet

In June 1776 Congress named a committee of five to draft a declaration. Jefferson did most of the writing, with help from Adams, Franklin, and others. Congress voted for independence on July 2 and approved the final words on July 4. After the famous opening paragraphs, the Declaration lists many grievancesGREE-vuntsA complaint about something unfair. against King George. Think of the list as a charge sheet. Each charge says: the king did this, and it violated a right.

Match a few. The Declaration says the king imposed “Taxes on us without our Consent.” That violates the right to property and the principle of consent of the governed. It says he deprived colonists “in many cases, of the benefits of Trial by Jury.” That touches the right to liberty and a fair trial. It says he kept “Standing Armies among us, in times of peace, without the Consent of our legislatures,” and quartered large bodies of troops among them. That threatens both liberty and the safety of homes. It says he dissolved colonial assemblies, which attacks the right of people to govern themselves through representatives.

Now you can see why the Declaration is more than an angry letter. It does not say only that the colonists dislike British rule. It argues, one charge at a time, that the king broke the contract that made his rule just. If the charges are true, then Locke’s right of revolution applies. That is why its opening paragraphs on natural rights come first. The rest of the document is the proof.

A good citizen should be able to judge such an argument. Ask of each charge whether it is true and whether it is serious enough to justify so grave a step. That is the question you will take up in this lesson’s discussion. The Declaration asks the reader to decide, as it asked “a candid world.”
