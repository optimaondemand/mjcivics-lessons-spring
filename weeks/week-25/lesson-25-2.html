Railroads, Steel, and Oil

After the Civil War the American economy grew very fast. Railroads tied the country together. Factories made steel, and cities spread. Writers later named the years from about 1870 to 1900 the Gilded Age. Gilded means covered with a thin layer of gold. Beneath the shine, they said, were serious troubles.

A few men became enormously rich. Andrew Carnegie built a steel empire. John D. Rockefeller started Standard Oil in 1870. He bought out rival oil refiners one after another and made secret deals with railroads to ship his oil cheaply. By about 1880, Standard Oil refined most of the oil in the country. Rockefeller and others joined their companies into trustsTRUSTA group of companies joined under one control so that they do not compete with each other., in which the companies no longer competed with one another.

A company with no real rivals is a monopolymuh-NOP-uh-leeControl of a market by one company, so that buyers have no real choice.. Think back to the lemonade stand. If one stand is the only one in town, it can raise the price as high as it likes, and its customers have nowhere else to go. It may also treat its workers and suppliers as it pleases. Competition is what pushes a business to be better. Without it, the market stops doing its job.

Rockefeller’s methods were harsh. When a rival refiner would not sell, Standard Oil might cut its prices in that town until the rival was ruined, and then raise them again once it was gone. Because it shipped so much oil, it also won lower freight rates from the railroads than smaller refiners could get. Rivals could not match those costs. Rockefeller and his defenders said that he made oil cheaper and more reliable for ordinary families, and it is true that kerosene prices fell. His critics answered that a business which can crush every rival will not keep prices low for long.

Journalists helped to turn public feeling. Between 1902 and 1904, Ida Tarbell published a long series of magazine articles on how Standard Oil had won its power. Readers across the country followed it. Many came to believe that something had to be done, and politicians took notice.

Farmers were among the first to feel it. They had to ship grain on railroads that charged them more than larger customers paid, and there was often only one railroad to choose. They demanded that government act.

Two Laws

Congress answered in two steps. In 1887 it passed the Interstate Commerce Act. In 1890 Congress passed the Sherman AntitrustAN-tee-trustDescribing laws meant to stop trusts and monopolies and to protect competition. Act. It was named for Senator John Sherman of Ohio. The law is short. It made it a crime to join together to restrain trade, and it made it a crime to monopolize a market. It was the nation’s first antitrustAN-tee-trustDescribing laws meant to stop trusts and monopolies and to protect competition. law.

At first the act did little. Courts read it narrowly, and officials were slow to use it. Then Theodore Roosevelt became President in 1901. He believed that the government must be stronger than the biggest companies. In 1906 his administration sued Standard Oil. In 1911 the Supreme Court ruled that the company was an illegal monopolymuh-NOP-uh-leeControl of a market by one company, so that buyers have no real choice. and ordered it broken into more than thirty separate companies.

The breakup did not destroy the oil business. The separate companies went on selling oil, and they now had to compete. Some of them grew into the great oil companies of today. The case showed that the republic could act against even the richest man in the country, and that no business is above the law. That idea was as important as the economic result.

Three years later, in 1914, Congress passed the Clayton Act. It named particular practices that hurt competition and made them illegal. It also created the Federal Trade Commissionkuh-MISH-unA group of officials appointed to carry out a special job for the government., an agencyAY-jen-seeA part of the government that carries out laws and does a particular job. that watches for unfair business practices. These laws are still in use today.

Why a Republic Worries

Why did a republic care so much? Partly because of prices. But there was a deeper reason. Classical writers such as Polybius and Cicero had warned that wealth can become a kind of power. A few very rich men can buy officials, write the laws, and decide who may compete. When wealth is gathered in a few hands, ordinary citizens lose their say.

This was also what Jefferson had feared. The American answer was not to ban business or to take it over. The answer was to keep the market open. The government set rules so that many sellers could compete and no one could rig the game. This is why the republic regulates competition. The goal is to keep economic power spread widely, so that political power stays spread widely too.

Not everyone agreed. Some said that big companies were more efficient and lowered prices. Others said that breaking them up punished success and that the market would correct itself. You will take up that argument in the discussion. As you do, ask what evidence each side would use.
