📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary

A few words worth knowing before you read.

command economy (kuh-MAND ih-KON-uh-mee)
An economy in which the government decides what is made, how much, and at what price.
competition (kom-puh-TISH-un)
The contest among sellers to win customers by offering better goods or lower prices.
manufacturing (man-yuh-FAK-chur-ing)
Making goods in large amounts, usually with machines.
market economy (MAR-kit ih-KON-uh-mee)
An economy in which people and businesses choose what to make, buy, and sell, and prices are set by buyers and sellers.
mixed economy (MIXT ih-KON-uh-mee)
An economy that is mostly a market but in which government also plays a part.
profit (PROF-it)
The money a business has left after it pays all its costs.
tariff (TAIR-if)
A tax on goods that are brought in from another country.

How a Market Works

In 1776, the year of the Declaration of Independence, a Scottish thinker named Adam Smith published a book called The Wealth of Nations. Smith asked why some countries are rich and others poor, and he looked at how ordinary people trade with one another.

Think about a lemonade stand. You choose a price. If the price is too high, no one buys. If it is too low, you run out of lemons and earn nothing. If another stand opens across the street with cheaper lemonade, you must lower your price or make yours better. That is competitionkom-puh-TISH-unThe contest among sellers to win customers by offering better goods or lower prices.. If you sell enough lemonade, you keep a profitPROF-itThe money a business has left after it pays all its costs., which is money left after your costs. Prices tell you what people want. ProfitPROF-itThe money a business has left after it pays all its costs. tells you to make more of it.

Smith saw that no one has to be in charge of this. A baker does not bake bread out of kindness. The baker wants to earn a living. But to earn it, the baker must make bread that people want to buy. Smith said that each person, thinking of his or her own gain, is led as if by an “invisible hand” to serve others. A country run this way has a market economyMAR-kit ih-KON-uh-meeAn economy in which people and businesses choose what to make, buy, and sell, and prices are set by buyers and sellers..

A market economyMAR-kit ih-KON-uh-meeAn economy in which people and businesses choose what to make, buy, and sell, and prices are set by buyers and sellers. differs from a command economykuh-MAND ih-KON-uh-meeAn economy in which the government decides what is made, how much, and at what price., in which the government decides what is made, how much of it, and what it costs. You will see in the coming days what happened when large nations tried that. In a market, millions of people decide for themselves. In a command economykuh-MAND ih-KON-uh-meeAn economy in which the government decides what is made, how much, and at what price., a few officials decide for everyone.

Prices also change when something becomes scarce. After a hurricane, plywood may be hard to find in Florida, and its price rises. The higher price tells builders in other states that plywood is wanted here, and it encourages them to send more. In time the price comes back down. No official gave an order. The price carried the message, and thousands of sellers answered it. That is the power Smith saw in a market.

Smith did not think that markets could run without rules. He wanted courts that would enforce contracts, and he wanted laws that kept people honest. A market needs rule of law, just as a republic does. Property has to be safe, and promises have to be kept.

📚 PART 2 · Two Visions for the Republic14 min

Hamilton and Jefferson Again

The new republic had to decide what kind of economy it wanted. In 1791 Alexander Hamilton sent Congress a Report on Manufacturesman-yuh-FAK-chur-ingMaking goods in large amounts, usually with machines.. He argued that America should not stay only a land of farms. Manufacturingman-yuh-FAK-chur-ingMaking goods in large amounts, usually with machines. would make the country stronger, give people more kinds of work, and free the nation from depending on foreign goods. He asked Congress to protect young factories with tariffsTAIR-ifA tax on goods that are brought in from another country. and to help industry in other ways.

Thomas Jefferson saw things differently. He believed that the best citizens were independent farmers who owned their own land. A man who owned his farm needed no one’s permission to vote his conscience. Factory workers, Jefferson feared, would depend on their bosses and could be pushed around, and big cities would breed the corruption that had sunk other republics.

Both men were thinking like classical republicans. The old question was what kind of people made a republic strong. Jefferson answered with farmers. Hamilton answered with a nation that was wealthy and well defended. The country has in fact had both, and it has argued about the balance ever since.

A Mixed Economy

In practice, the United States did not choose a pure market or a pure command economykuh-MAND ih-KON-uh-meeAn economy in which the government decides what is made, how much, and at what price.. It built a mixed economyMIXT ih-KON-uh-meeAn economy that is mostly a market but in which government also plays a part.. Private people and businesses make most of the decisions, but government also plays a part. Government builds roads and harbors, sets the money system, runs schools, enforces contracts, and taxes imports.

⚙️ Technology: The Erie Canal

The Erie Canal was dug by hand and horse from Albany to Buffalo, a distance of about 363 miles. It opened in 1825. The state of New York paid for it. Shipping a ton of goods from Buffalo to New York City fell from about a hundred dollars to about ten. Towns and farms sprang up along its path.

The best example is the Erie Canal. Before it was built, the mountains made it costly to send goods from the Great Lakes to the sea. The state of New York borrowed the money and built a canal. Governor DeWitt Clinton pushed the plan through, even though critics called it “Clinton’s Ditch.” When the canal opened in 1825, the cost of shipping dropped sharply. Farmers in the Midwest could sell grain in the East, and New York City became the country’s busiest port.

TariffsTAIR-ifA tax on goods that are brought in from another country. were another tool. The first Congress passed a tariffTAIR-ifA tax on goods that are brought in from another country. in 1789, and it brought in most of the money the government needed in its early years. It also made foreign goods cost more, which helped American makers of cloth, iron, and tools competekom-puh-TISH-unThe contest among sellers to win customers by offering better goods or lower prices. at home. Not everyone gained. A farmer who bought tools at a higher price paid more because of the tariffTAIR-ifA tax on goods that are brought in from another country.. Here again, Americans argued about who should gain and who should pay, and that argument is part of living in a mixed economyMIXT ih-KON-uh-meeAn economy that is mostly a market but in which government also plays a part..

Geography helped explain where industry grew. Coal, iron ore, timber, and fast rivers were the raw materials and power of early factories. Pennsylvania had coal and iron. New England had rivers that could turn mill wheels. Ports, canals, and later railroads carried the goods to market. Where resources met transportation, cities grew.

A mixed economyMIXT ih-KON-uh-meeAn economy that is mostly a market but in which government also plays a part. helped build a democratic nation, and not only a rich one. A market spread ownership widely, and it gave ordinary people the chance to start a farm, a shop, or a business. Government projects such as canals and schools opened that chance to more people. Citizens who owned something had a stake in the republic. That is why Americans often judge an economic idea not only by how much wealth it makes but also by whether it keeps citizens free and independent.
