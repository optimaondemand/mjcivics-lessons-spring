📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary

A few words worth knowing before you read.

Being put on trial a second time for a crime after a person was already found not guilty of it.
due process (DOO PRAH-ses)
The rule that government must follow fair and regular legal steps before it takes away a person’s life, liberty, or property.
incorporation (in-kor-per-AY-shun)

naturalization (nach-er-ul-ih-ZAY-shun)
The process by which a person born in another country becomes a citizen.
self-incrimination (self-in-krim-ih-NAY-shun)

warrant (WOR-ent)
A paper signed by a judge that gives police permission to search a place or arrest a person.

Duties and Responsibilities

You have followed the citizen through much of this course. A citizen is a full member of the republic, one of its part-owners. In the United States a person becomes a citizen in one of two ways. The first is birth. The 14th Amendment says that anyone born in the country and under its laws is a citizen. The second is naturalizationnach-er-ul-ih-ZAY-shunThe process by which a person born in another country becomes a citizen., in which a person born elsewhere follows rules set by Congress and takes an oath. Either way, a citizen holds rights and also owes something back.

What a citizen owes comes in two kinds. Some things are duties, which the law requires. Citizens must obey the laws, pay taxes, and serve on a jury when called. Young men must register for the draft, called Selective Service, when they turn eighteen. Children must attend school. Other things are responsibilities, which a free citizen chooses to do. These include voting, staying informed, volunteering, and speaking up about problems. No law forces you to do them. But a republic that has only duties and no responsibilities will slowly weaken, because no one is minding the common good.

Jury service shows both ideas together. When a person is accused of a serious crime, a grand jury of citizens first decides whether there is enough evidence to bring a charge. At the trial, a second jury of citizens hears the evidence. The government must prove guilt beyond a reasonable doubt, and in federal criminal trials the verdict must be unanimous. A juror is doing the work of ruling, as the Athenians once did. It is a duty, and the best jurors treat it as an honor.

📚 PART 2 · The Bill of Rights, Group by Group13 min

Ten Amendments, Five Groups

Many people who opposed the Constitution in 1788 said they would accept it only if it had a bill of rights. The Federalists promised to add one. James Madison wrote the amendments, and by December 1791 the states had ratified ten of them. They are called the Bill of Rights. Their main purpose is to protect individuals and minorities from the power of the government. It helps to sort them into groups.

The first group protects the freedom of mind and voice. The First Amendment says Congress may not set up an official religion or ban the free practice of religion. It also protects freedom of speech and of the press, the right to gather peacefully, and the right to petition the government for change. The Second Amendment protects the right to keep and bear arms. The Third says soldiers may not be placed in a private home in peacetime without the owner’s consent. The colonists remembered what that was like.

The next group protects people who are accused of crimes. The Fourth Amendment forbids unreasonable searches and seizures and says police must usually have a warrantWOR-entA paper signed by a judge that gives police permission to search a place or arrest a person., based on good reason, to search. The Fifth Amendment guarantees due processDOO PRAH-sesThe rule that government must follow fair and regular legal steps before it takes away a person’s life, liberty, or property.. It also says no one may be tried twice for the same crime, which is called double jeopardyDUB-ul JEP-er-deeBeing put on trial a second time for a crime after a person was already found not guilty of it., and no one may be forced to testify against themselves, which is called self-incriminationself-in-krim-ih-NAY-shunBeing forced to say something that could prove you guilty of a crime.. The Sixth gives the accused a speedy and public trial, an impartial jury, the right to hear the witnesses, and the right to a lawyer. The Seventh protects jury trials in many civil cases, and the Eighth forbids excessive bail and cruel and unusual punishment.

The last group says the list is not the whole story. The Ninth Amendment says that the people keep other rights even if they are not named. The Tenth, which you have already studied, says that powers not given to the nation belong to the states or the people. Together these two keep the Bill of Rights from being read as a complete list of everything a free people may do.

📚 PART 3 · The 14th Amendment Reaches the States12 min

Who Must Obey the Bill of Rights?

At first the Bill of Rights limited only the national government. In 1833 the Supreme Court said so directly, in a case about a wharf in Baltimore. A state or city could break these rights, and the Bill of Rights did not stop it. Then came the Civil War and the 14th Amendment. It says that no state may take a person’s life, liberty, or property without due processDOO PRAH-sesThe rule that government must follow fair and regular legal steps before it takes away a person’s life, liberty, or property. of law, and no state may deny any person equal protection of the laws.

Over many years the Supreme Court read those words to mean that most of the Bill of Rights also binds the states. This step is called incorporationin-kor-per-AY-shunThe process by which the Supreme Court applied most of the Bill of Rights to state governments through the 14th Amendment.. In 1925 the Court said that the First Amendment’s protection of free speech applied to the states. In 1963, in Gideon v. Wainwright, it said that a state must provide a lawyer to a poor person charged with a serious crime. Because of incorporationin-kor-per-AY-shunThe process by which the Supreme Court applied most of the Bill of Rights to state governments through the 14th Amendment., a state police officer must follow the Fourth Amendment, a state court must follow the Sixth, and a city council cannot silence a newspaper.

Notice how the answer to our opening question grows. Rights protect individuals and minorities from the majority. The national and state governments are bound to respect them, and the courts decide what that means. And citizens must know their rights to claim them. A right that no one knows about is easy to take away.
