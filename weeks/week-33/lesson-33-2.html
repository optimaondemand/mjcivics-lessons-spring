📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
How can courts change the law without changing the words of the Constitution?
The words of the Constitution change rarely, but their meaning is tested in court case after court case. This lesson follows six cases and the amendments that widened who takes part in the republic.

📚 PART 1 · Religion, Searches, and Arrests13 min
📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary

A few words worth knowing before you read.

dissent (dih-SENT)
An opinion written by a judge who disagrees with the court’s decision.
interrogation (in-ter-uh-GAY-shun)
Questioning of a suspect by police.
precedent (PRES-ih-dent)
An earlier court decision that judges use as a guide in deciding similar cases later.
reasonable suspicion (REE-zun-uh-bul sus-PISH-un)
A good reason, based on facts, to think that a person may be breaking the law.
segregation (seg-rih-GAY-shun)
The forced separation of people by race in schools, trains, and other public places.

Cases Under the First Amendment

Judges decide real quarrels between real people. When their decisions explain a rule, other courts follow them. A decision used as a guide in later cases is a precedentPRES-ih-dentAn earlier court decision that judges use as a guide in deciding similar cases later.. Through precedentPRES-ih-dentAn earlier court decision that judges use as a guide in deciding similar cases later., the courts shape what a clause means over time, even though the clause itself stays the same. Six cases show how.

Two concern religion. In Lemon v. Kurtzman, in 1971, Pennsylvania paid part of the salaries of teachers in religious schools. The Court said this went too far, and it set out a test: a law about religion must have a purpose that is not religious, and it must not help or hurt religion or tangle government up with it. In Sherbert v. Verner, in 1963, a woman lost her job because she would not work on Saturday, the day of worship in her church. South Carolina then refused her unemployment pay. The Court said the state could not make her choose between her faith and her benefits. The first case limited government’s support of religion. The second protected a person’s freedom to practice it.

Cases About Police

Two more cases deal with police and the Fourth Amendment. In Terry v. Ohio, in 1968, a Cleveland officer watched two men walk past a store window again and again, as if planning a robbery. He stopped them and patted down their coats, and found guns. The Court said an officer may briefly stop and search a person for weapons when he has reasonable suspicionREE-zun-uh-bul sus-PISH-unA good reason, based on facts, to think that a person may be breaking the law.. A hunch is not enough. The case tried to balance the safety of officers and the public against a person’s right to be left alone.

The next case concerns the Fifth and Sixth Amendments. In 1963 police in Phoenix arrested Ernesto Miranda. During two hours of interrogationin-ter-uh-GAY-shunQuestioning of a suspect by police. he confessed, but no one had told him that he could stay silent or ask for a lawyer. In Miranda v. Arizona, in 1966, the Court said a confession is not allowed in court unless police first warn a suspect of these rights. That is why police today say, “You have the right to remain silent.” Miranda was tried again without his confession and convicted anyway. The ruling did not set criminals free. It made sure police follow fair steps.

📚 PART 2 · Plessy, Harlan, and Brown13 min

Separate but Equal

The two cases that follow concern the 14th Amendment’s promise of equal protection. After Reconstruction ended, many southern states passed laws requiring segregationseg-rih-GAY-shunThe forced separation of people by race in schools, trains, and other public places.. Louisiana ordered separate railway cars for white and Black passengers. Homer Plessy, a man of mixed ancestry, sat in a car for white passengers on purpose, to test the law. He was arrested. In Plessy v. Ferguson, in 1896, the Supreme Court upheld the law by a vote of seven to one. The Court said that equal protection did not mean that the races had to mix, so long as the separate facilities were equal. The phrase “separate but equal” became law for nearly sixty years.

One justice saw it differently. John Marshall Harlan wrote a lone dissentdih-SENTAn opinion written by a judge who disagrees with the court’s decision.. He said, “Our Constitution is color-blind, and neither knows nor tolerates classes among citizens.” He warned that the law told Black Americans they were not fit to ride with whites, and that it would cause lasting harm. Harlan lost. But think of what might have happened if the majority had agreed with him in 1896. Segregationseg-rih-GAY-shunThe forced separation of people by race in schools, trains, and other public places. laws might have fallen decades earlier, and the long struggle that followed might have taken a very different shape. His words became the road not taken, and later lawyers returned to it.

Brown Overturns Plessy

In 1954 the Court heard Brown v. Board of Education, which joined cases from several states. Lawyers for the families, led by Thurgood Marshall, argued that separate schools could never be equal. Chief Justice Earl Warren wrote for a unanimous Court that separate schools are “inherently unequal.” The Court overturned Plessy in schools. That is rare. Courts usually follow precedentPRES-ih-dentAn earlier court decision that judges use as a guide in deciding similar cases later.. But a court may reverse an earlier ruling when it concludes that the ruling was wrong.

📚 PART 3 · Widening the Circle12 min

The Participation Amendments in Order

Cases are one way the republic changes. Amendments are another. Earlier in the course you followed six amendments that widened who takes part. In order, they are the 13th, in 1865, which ended slavery; the 14th, in 1868, which made citizens of those born here and promised equal protection; the 15th, in 1870, which said the vote could not be denied because of race; the 19th, in 1920, which gave women the vote; the 24th, in 1964, which ended the poll tax in federal elections; and the 26th, in 1971, which lowered the voting age to eighteen.

Put the cases and amendments side by side. The 14th Amendment made a promise in 1868. Plessy weakened it in 1896. Brown began to keep it in 1954, and the amendments of 1964 and 1971 widened the vote further. Each step worked through the republic’s own rules, by argument, ballots, and the courts, and not around them.

This also answers our opening question. Courts change how the Constitution works in practice by deciding cases and by following or reversing precedentPRES-ih-dentAn earlier court decision that judges use as a guide in deciding similar cases later.. They cannot change the text. Only the people can do that, through the amendment process. For a citizen, this means two roads for protecting a right. One runs through the courts, and the other through the ballot and the amendment door.
