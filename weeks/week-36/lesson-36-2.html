📚 PART 1 · Roles, Rights, and Responsibilities12 min
📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary

A few words worth knowing before you read.

policy (POL-ih-see)
A plan or set of rules a government follows to deal with a problem.
transfer of power (TRANS-fer of POW-er)
The handing over of government leadership from one person or party to another.

What a Citizen Does

The second practice section covers what citizens do and how public policyPOL-ih-seeA plan or set of rules a government follows to deal with a problem. is made. Start with the citizen. A citizen is born in the country or naturalized, holds the rights in the Bill of Rights, and owes duties that the law requires, such as obeying laws, paying taxes, and serving on a jury. A free citizen also takes on responsibilities that no law demands, such as voting, staying informed, and volunteering. The 14th Amendment carried the Bill of Rights to the states, and landmark cases such as Miranda and Brown show how courts apply those rights.

Next, remember the policyPOL-ih-seeA plan or set of rules a government follows to deal with a problem. process. An election runs through registration, primaries, a general election, and a count, and the President is chosen by the Electoral College. Media, interest groups, and ordinary citizens watch and influence government, and a careful citizen checks any source for bias. Foreign policyPOL-ih-seeA plan or set of rules a government follows to deal with a problem. is tied to life at home, and treaties need two thirds of the Senate. The economy is a mixed one, with taxes, credit, and competition laws under the rule of law, and the evidence from GDP per capita favors free markets over command economies.

Remember also the local-problem method. A citizen researches the problem, finds the agency in charge, weighs the alternatives, and chooses an action. The same steps work for a pothole, a school rule, or a state law, and they show how the ballot, the press, the interest group, and the petition are one power used at different scales. Expect practice questions that give you a small problem and ask which agency or action fits best.

Do not try to memorize this paragraph. Use it as a checklist. For each topic, ask whether you can explain it in two or three sentences without looking. The ones you cannot are the ones to study.

📚 PART 3 · Two Republics, Two Outcomes14 min

The Question Behind the Course

Now return to the question that has run through the course. What keeps a republic alive after it is built? Two stories answer it. In 1800 the American republic was young, divided, and angry. Federalists and Republicans accused each other of wanting to destroy the country. The election ended in a tie, and the House needed thirty-six ballots to choose a President. Yet Thomas Jefferson was chosen, John Adams left office, and the transfer of powerTRANS-fer of POW-erThe handing over of government leadership from one person or party to another. happened without a shot. Then the country fixed the flaw with the 12th Amendment.

In 1933 the German republic had a modern constitution, a parliament, and courts. But it was also weak. Defeat in war, inflation, and the Great Depression made many Germans angry and afraid. On January 30, 1933, President Hindenburg appointed Adolf Hitler chancellor. Within weeks, the new government used the republic’s own emergency rules to take away rights, and in March it passed the Enabling Act, which let it make laws without the parliament. Within a year, the republic was gone.

What made the difference? Not the written constitutions alone, since Germany had one. Part of the answer lies in the failures the Framers feared. The American system spread power among branches and between nation and states. It protected rights in writing. Its leaders, flawed as they were, accepted the result of an election. And its citizens, in large numbers, believed in the rules. In Germany, many leaders and voters abandoned the rules when they felt desperate, and the safeguards could not hold. These stories are warnings of choices, not of fate. Every republic faces such a moment, and what happens depends on the people in it.

Your Last Question

In your notebook, answer the discussion question in your own words: which part of the Constitution answers which failure? Choose three parts, and for each one name the failure it answers and one example from the course. Then say which of the three you think a republic most needs in a time of crisis, and why.
