📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
What did it take to widen the vote, one amendment at a time?
The 15th Amendment did not give every citizen the ballot. Three later amendments finished more of the work. Each one took years of argument, and each one worked inside the rules.

Seneca Falls, 1848

In July 1848, about three hundred people met in Seneca Falls, New York. Elizabeth Cady Stanton and Lucretia Mott had called the meeting to talk about the rights of women. Stanton wrote a document for it, the Declaration of SentimentsSEN-tih-mentA feeling or opinion about something.. It was written on the model of the Declaration of Independence, and the copy was deliberate. The writers wanted to show that the American promise of liberty was meant for women too.

The Declaration of Independence says, “all men are created equal.” The Declaration of SentimentsSEN-tih-mentA feeling or opinion about something. says, “all men and women are created equal.” Like Jefferson’s document, it listed grievances. It said that women could not vote, could not hold most property once they married, and could not enter most schools or trades. It asked for suffrageSUF-rijThe right to vote.. Frederick Douglass, who was there, spoke up for the demand when others hesitated. The call for the vote was the boldest demand, and it was the one that took longest to win.

The Long Argument

Women then argued for decades. Susan B. Anthony traveled the country to speak. In 1872 she voted in a national election to test the law, and she was arrested and fined. She died in 1906, fourteen years before the amendment was passed. In the 1910s a new group led by Alice Paul held marches and stood outside the White House with signs. Some were jailed for it, and some went on hunger strikes in prison.

Some places moved first and gave women the vote on their own. Wyoming did so as a territory in 1869. Yet the movement wanted a rule for the whole nation. That meant an amendment. Congress finally proposed the 19th Amendment in June 1919. It needed thirty-six states to ratifyRAT-ih-fyTo approve something officially so that it becomes law. it. By the summer of 1920, thirty-five had done so, and the last one needed was Tennessee.

📚 PART 2 · The Last Vote and Two More Amendments12 min

Tennessee, August 1920

The vote in the Tennessee House was close. Supporters and opponents crowded the capitol in Nashville. Harry Burn, at twenty-four the youngest member, had worn a red rose that showed he was against the amendment. Then he voted yes. The final count was 49 to 47. Burn said later that a letter from his mother had changed his mind. She had told him to support the women’s cause.

One vote changed the story. If Burn had voted no, the amendment might have stalled for months or longer. Tennessee ratifiedRAT-ih-fyTo approve something officially so that it becomes law. the amendment, and by the end of August 1920 it was the law. Women had won the vote after seventy-two years of argument, and they won it by the same road the earlier amendments used.

The amendment is short. It says the right to vote “shall not be denied or abridged” because of sex. It did not fix everything. In much of the South, Black women ran into the same poll taxesPOHL taksA fee that a person had to pay before being allowed to vote., tests, and threats that kept Black men from the ballot. The amendment widened the republic, but the work was not done.

The 24th Amendment, 1964

The 15th Amendment banned denying the vote because of race, but states had found other ways. One was the poll taxPOHL taksA fee that a person had to pay before being allowed to vote.. A voter had to pay a fee, and many poor citizens, Black and white, could not afford it. In 1964 the nation ratifiedRAT-ih-fyTo approve something officially so that it becomes law. the 24th Amendment. It banned the poll taxPOHL taksA fee that a person had to pay before being allowed to vote. in elections for federal office. Only a handful of Southern states still used it by then. Two years later the Supreme Court said that poll taxesPOHL taksA fee that a person had to pay before being allowed to vote. in state elections were also unconstitutional.

The 26th Amendment, 1971

The last amendment of the three came out of a war. In the 1960s the United States drafted young men to fight in Vietnam. Many of them were eighteen, and they were too young to vote. People said, “Old enough to fight, old enough to vote.” Earlier in this course you studied President Johnson’s speech defending the war. The war made the question of age hard to ignore.

In 1970 Congress had passed a law setting the voting age at eighteen for all elections. The Supreme Court said Congress could do that only for federal elections. States would have needed two sets of ballots, so the nation chose an amendment. Congress proposed the 26th Amendment in March 1971. The states ratifiedRAT-ih-fyTo approve something officially so that it becomes law. it in about one hundred days, faster than any amendment before it. It lowered the voting age to eighteen across the nation.

What the Four Share

Look back at the four amendments you have now seen: the 15th, 19th, 24th, and 26th. None of them was handed down from above. Each widened the republic by working through the republic’s own rules, with proposals in Congress and votes in the states. That shows a republic can widen its people without breaking itself.

Was the slow pace a fault or a safeguard? Fair people disagree. The same rules that made these changes so slow also made them hard to undo. Nobody has ever repealed an amendment that widened the vote.

It also shows what citizens have to do. The women of Seneca Falls spoke up. Anthony voted and was fined for it. Paul stood outside in the cold. Burn listened to his mother. The ballot had to be argued for, one person at a time.
