📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
How can a republic change its own rules without breaking them?
The Framers knew they were not perfect. They gave the Constitution a road for change. The people the Constitution first left out used that road.

Hard, but Possible

The Framers did not think they had written a perfect Constitution. They also did not want it changed every time a crowd felt angry. So they wrote Article V, which sets out how to amend the Constitution. The road is hard, but it can be traveled.

There are two steps. First, an amendment must be proposed. Two-thirds of both houses of Congress can propose it. The other way is a convention called by two-thirds of the states, which has never been used. Second, the amendment must be ratifiedRAT-ih-fyTo approve something officially so that it becomes law.. Three-fourths of the states must approve it. That is why a small group cannot change the Constitution alone. The President does not sign an amendment, and it goes straight to the states. Thousands of amendments have been proposed, but only twenty-seven have been ratifiedRAT-ih-fyTo approve something officially so that it becomes law..

The Framers remembered the Articles of Confederation, which could be changed only if every state agreed. That rule had trapped the country. Article V is a middle path. It is hard enough to protect the Constitution from passing moods. It is open enough that a determined people can still change it.

The 13th Amendment, 1865

The first test came in the Civil War. In 1864 the Senate passed an amendment to end slavery. The House did not give it the two-thirds it needed. Then Abraham Lincoln won reelection in November. Congress still had a few months left before the new members arrived, so it was a lame-duckLAYM dukAn official or a Congress that is still in office but has been voted out or is about to leave. Congress. Lincoln pressed it to act before the term ended, and his allies worked on members one by one.

On January 31, 1865, the House voted 119 to 56. That was only a handful of votes more than the two-thirds required. Some members changed their minds. Others stayed away and let the vote pass. The amendment went to the states. Lincoln was killed in April, before the states had finished. In December 1865 it became part of the Constitution.

Think about how close this was. If the House had failed again, the question would have waited for the next Congress, and the war might have ended in a different way. The 13th Amendment says that neither slavery nor involuntary servitude may exist in the United States, except as punishment for a crime.

The Black Codes and the 14th Amendment

The war was over, but the argument was not. The nation entered Reconstructionree-kun-STRUK-shunThe years after the Civil War when the nation tried to rebuild the South and decide what rights freed people would have.. In 1865 and 1866, Southern states passed Black CodesBLAK kohdzLaws passed in Southern states right after the war that took away many rights from freed Black Americans.. These laws limited where freed people could work and live. Some barred them from testifying against white people in court. Some let officers arrest a Black worker who had no job and force him to work for a landowner. They were a way to keep freedom in name only.

Congress answered with the 14th Amendment, ratifiedRAT-ih-fyTo approve something officially so that it becomes law. in 1868. You met it earlier in this module as the rule that makes birth a road to citizenship. It also forbids a state to take life, liberty, or property without due process, and it promises every person the equal protection of the laws. These words aimed at the Black CodesBLAK kohdzLaws passed in Southern states right after the war that took away many rights from freed Black Americans., and they were meant to bind the states. Southern states also had to ratifyRAT-ih-fyTo approve something officially so that it becomes law. it before they could return to Congress.

The 15th Amendment, 1870

Citizenship was one thing. The vote was another. In 1870 the nation ratifiedRAT-ih-fyTo approve something officially so that it becomes law. the 15th Amendment. It says that the right to vote cannot be denied “on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude.” Frederick Douglass, who had escaped slavery and become a famous speaker and writer, had argued for this for years. He believed that a free man without a vote was not safe.

For a short time the amendment worked. Black men voted, ran for office, and won. Hiram Revels of Mississippi became the first Black member of the Senate in 1870. But when Reconstructionree-kun-STRUK-shunThe years after the Civil War when the nation tried to rebuild the South and decide what rights freed people would have. ended in 1877, federal troops left the South, and the protection left with them.

Then Southern states looked for ways around the amendment. The 15th Amendment forbade denying the vote because of race. It did not forbid a poll taxPOHL taksA fee that a person had to pay before being allowed to vote. or a literacy testLIT-ur-uh-see testA test of reading and writing that a person had to pass before being allowed to vote.. The laws did not say “Black.” Yet officials gave the tests unfairly, and the fees were too high for many poor families. Violence did the rest. The words of the amendment stood, but its promise was broken in practice.

Notice, too, who was still left out. The 15th Amendment said nothing about sex. Women of every race could not vote in most of the country. Their story is the next part of this lesson.

You have already seen how the Court dealt with this period. In Plessy v. Ferguson it allowed separate public places, and for many decades it did not stop the evasions. In Brown v. Board of Education it finally said that separate schools were unequal. The 14th Amendment’s promise was kept slowly and at a high cost.

Notice what these three amendments teach. The nation did not tear up the Constitution to widen the republic. It used Article V. The change was hard won, and the evasions were hard fought. But every step began as an argument about what the people’s own rules should say.

💪 CARRY THIS FORWARD

The Constitution can be changed, but only when two-thirds of Congress and three-fourths of the states are persuaded. That road made the republic wider after the Civil War. It also shows that a promise on paper still needs citizens who will insist that it be kept.
