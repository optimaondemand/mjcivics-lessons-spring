📚 PART 1 · Athens: Citizens Who Ruled12 min

A Review That Starts With a Pattern

This course opened with a question: who should rule, and what makes that rule fair? Now you will go back over the whole answer, and you will notice a pattern. Almost every idea the American Founders used had been tried somewhere before. Some of those tries worked. Some failed badly. The Founders studied the failures as closely as the successes, and that is a large part of why the Constitution looks the way it does.

🌐 Foreign Language Vocabulary
polis (POH-lis)
Greek for “city-state.” A Greek town and its land that governed itself.
res publica (rays POO-blih-kah)
Latin for “the public thing.” The Roman name for their republic, and the root of our word “republic.”

Begin with Greece. The Greeks lived in small, independent towns, and each ran its own affairs. A Greek city-state (polisPOH-lisGreek for “city-state.” A Greek town and its land that governed itself.) might be only a town and the farms around it. Our words “politics” and “political” come from polisPOH-lisGreek for “city-state.” A Greek town and its land that governed itself.. The most famous polisPOH-lisGreek for “city-state.” A Greek town and its land that governed itself. was Athens.

In Athens the citizens ruled directly. Every free adult man born to Athenian parents could attend the Assembly, speak, and vote on laws and on war. A Council of five hundred citizens, chosen by lot, prepared the business. Ordinary citizens also served as jurors in large courts. Taking part was seen as a duty, not just a right, and a man who stayed out of public life was thought to be failing his city. The laws were written down and posted in public, so that no ruler could change them in secret.

But the circle of citizens was small. Women, enslaved people, and foreigners could not vote. You have seen how much of American history is the story of widening such a circle.

Athens also shows how free government can be lost. In 404 BC, after Athens lost a long war to Sparta, a small group of rich men took control and ruled by fear. That is an oligarchyOL-ih-gar-keeRule by a small group, often the rich., rule by the few. Within a year the citizens drove the oligarchsOL-ih-gar-keeRule by a small group, often the rich. out and brought back the Assembly. The lesson cuts two ways. Free government can be lost in a bad year, and it can be won back by people willing to fight for it. The Founders also noticed how a crowd voting on everything could swing from one mood to another. They chose to have citizens elect representatives instead.

📚 PART 2 · Rome: A Republic Built to Last13 min

The Public Thing

The Romans called their government the public thing (res publicarays POO-blih-kahLatin for “the public thing.” The Roman name for their republic, and the root of our word “republic.”). The words mean that the government belonged to the public and not to a king. From them we get our word “republic.” Roman citizens did not vote on every law the way Athenians did. They elected officials to act for them. Each year they chose two consuls to lead the government and the army, judges called praetors, and tribunes whose job was to protect ordinary citizens. This is representative government: citizens choose people to act in their name.

Written Law

In the early years of the Republic, poor Romans complained that the judges, who were all nobles, bent the law however they liked. Around 450 BC the Romans answered by writing the laws on tablets, called the Twelve Tables, and setting them up in the public square. Anyone, rich or poor, could now read what the law said. This is the rule of law: laws that are known in advance and that bind everyone, including those who make them.

A Mixed Constitution

A Greek historian named Polybius tried to explain why Rome grew so strong. He said that Rome mixed three kinds of government. The consuls were like a king, one person leading. The Senate was like an aristocracy, a few of the wisest and most experienced. The assemblies of citizens were like a democracy, the many. Each part could hold back the other two, so no one part could take everything. This is the idea of a mixed constitution, and it points toward the separation of powers, in which different people hold different jobs of government.

But Rome failed too, and the Founders knew how. When ambitious generals commanded armies that were loyal to them and not to the law, the offices of the Republic could no longer hold them back. Caesar and then Augustus took the power. The offices stayed, and the substance was gone. A balanced government is not safe by itself. It lasts only while citizens care more for their laws than for a leader.

📚 PART 3 · Jerusalem: Law Over Kings13 min

The Third Root

The third root of American ideas grew in Jerusalem. The Judeo-Christian tradition means the beliefs of Jews and Christians, shaped by the Bible. Several of its teachings reached the Founders. The first is that every person is made in the image of God. This gives each person dignityDIG-nih-teeThe worth that every person has, no matter who they are., a worth that no ruler gives and no ruler can take away.

The second teaching is that law stands above kings. The Bible tells how King David did a great wrong. He took another man’s wife and arranged to have the man killed. A prophetPROF-itA person who speaks for God, often to warn rulers and people. named Nathan came to the king and told him plainly that he had sinned. Even the most powerful man in Israel had to answer to a law higher than his own wishes. The Founders took this idea and used it against King George, and in the Constitution against any ruler.

The third teaching is personal responsibility. Each person answers for the choices he or she makes, and cannot blame the crowd or the king. Christians added the teaching that all people are equal before God, and that what is owed to Caesar is different from what is owed to God. This meant that government, however large, is not everything. A citizen has a conscience that the state may not own.

What the Founders Kept

Now put the three roots together. From Athens, the Founders took the idea that citizens take part in ruling. From Rome, they took elected representatives, written law, and a government in balanced parts. From Jerusalem, they took the worth of every person and law that binds kings. You can hear all three in the Declaration of Independence, which says that all men are created equal and are endowed by their Creator with rights. You can see them in the Constitution, with its elected Congress, its three branches, and its written rules.

Remember also what each root teaches about failure. Athens lost its government to a few rich men. Rome lost its republic to generals. Both lost it when the people stopped guarding it. The Founders did not simply copy these places. They tried to repair what had gone wrong in each. The rest of this quarter follows those repairs.
