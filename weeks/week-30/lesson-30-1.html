📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
What was wrong with the first American government, and how did the Constitution repair it?
The Declaration said what Americans believed. It did not say how to govern a nation. The first plan failed, and the failure taught the lessons that shaped the Constitution.

📚 PART 1 · A Government That Could Not Govern13 min
📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary

A few words worth knowing before you read.

compromise (KOM-pruh-mize)
An agreement in which each side gives up something to reach a deal.
confederation (kun-fed-er-AY-shun)
A loose union of states in which the central government has little power.
convention (kun-VEN-shun)
A meeting of delegates called for a special purpose.
tranquility (tran-KWIL-ih-tee)
Calm and peace.

The First Plan

When the colonies declared independence, they needed a government to fight the war. Congress wrote a plan called the Articles of Confederationkun-fed-er-AY-shunA loose union of states in which the central government has little power., which the states finished approving in 1781. The Americans had just broken free of a king, and they feared strong rulers. So they built a confederationkun-fed-er-AY-shunA loose union of states in which the central government has little power., a loose union of thirteen states in which almost all power stayed with the states. The Articles had real strengths. They held the states together through the war, and they gave the nation a way to make treaties. The Articles also gave the country the Northwest Ordinance, a plan for new states in the west.

Five Weaknesses

Still, the Articles had grave weaknesses, and the first was money. Congress could not tax. It could only ask the states to send money, and the states often did not. The government could not even pay the soldiers who had won the war.

The second weakness was that there was no President or any other executive to carry out the laws. Congress had to act through committees. The third was that there were no national courts. A dispute between two states had no judge to settle it. The fourth was that Congress could not control trade. States taxed each other’s goods, as if they were foreign nations, and Britain and Spain took advantage of the division.

The fifth weakness was that the Articles were almost impossible to change. An amendment needed all thirteen states to agree, so a single state could block it. Even ordinary laws needed nine of the thirteen states to approve. A government that cannot raise money, enforce laws, settle disputes, or fix itself will not last long. Americans would soon learn this the hard way.

📚 PART 2 · A Rebellion and a Convention13 min

Shays’ Rebellion

In 1786 the farmers of western Massachusetts were in trouble. Prices for their crops were low, and the state demanded high taxes to pay its war debts. Courts seized farms from those who could not pay. A former army captain named Daniel Shays led hundreds of angry farmers to shut down the courts so that no more farms could be taken. In January 1787 they marched on the arsenal at Springfield to seize weapons.

The government of Massachusetts had to hire a private army, paid for by wealthy citizens, to put the rebels down. Congress under the Articles could do nothing. It had no money and almost no army. Leaders such as George Washington were alarmed. They feared that if a weak government could not keep order, ordinary people would soon turn to a strong man to restore it. It was the old story of Athens and Rome.

Philadelphia, 1787

In the summer of 1787, delegates from twelve states met in Philadelphia at a conventionkun-VEN-shunA meeting of delegates called for a special purpose.. Rhode Island refused to send anyone. They had been called only to fix the Articles, but they soon decided to write a new plan. Washington, the most trusted man in America, was chosen to preside. James Madison, a young Virginian who had studied governments for years, brought a plan and took careful notes. The delegates met in secret all summer, with the windows shut in the heat.

The biggest quarrel was about representation. Large states wanted seats in Congress by population. Small states wanted each state to have the same number. The delegates settled it with a compromiseKOM-pruh-mizeAn agreement in which each side gives up something to reach a deal.. Congress would have two houses. In the House of Representatives, each state would have seats according to its population. In the Senate, each state would have two senators. 📚 PART 3 · The Preamble as a Repair List12 min

Reading It as a List of Cures

Now reread the Preamble the way the delegates meant it. Each purpose in it answers one of the failures you just studied. It says “to form a more perfect Union.” The states had been behaving like separate countries, and the Constitution joined them under one national government. It says “establish Justice.” The Articles had no national courts, so the Constitution created them. It says “insure domestic Tranquilitytran-KWIL-ih-teeCalm and peace..” Shays’ Rebellion had shown that the nation could not keep the peace at home. The Constitution gave the national government the power to do it.

It says “provide for the common defence.” The Articles had given Congress almost no army and no money to pay one. The Constitution made the President commander in chief and gave Congress the power to raise armies and to tax. It says “promote the general Welfare.” Under the Articles, states had taxed each other’s goods and hurt one another’s trade. The Constitution gave Congress the power to regulate trade among the states. And it says “secure the Blessings of Liberty,” which echoes the Declaration.

Weakness Matched to Remedy

Let us match each weakness with its remedy. Congress could not tax: the Constitution gave Congress the power to tax in Article I. There was no executive: Article II created a President. There were no national courts: Article III created a Supreme Court and let Congress set up lower ones. States hurt trade: Article I gave Congress the power to regulate commerce. The Articles could be changed only by all thirteen states: Article V lets three fourths of the states approve an amendment.

Notice that the Founders did not simply swing to the opposite extreme. They had learned from kings that too much power is dangerous, and they had learned from the Articles that too little power is dangerous too. So they built a government strong enough to act and divided into parts so that no part could rule alone. That balance is the thesis of the Constitution. Before it could take effect, the people of the states had to accept it, and they did not agree easily, as the next lesson shows.
