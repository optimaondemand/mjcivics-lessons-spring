📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Who was right about the Constitution: the people who wanted it as written, or the people who wanted a bill of rights first?
Writing the Constitution was only half the work. Nine states had to agree to it, and many Americans feared it. Their argument gave us the Bill of Rights and teaches how law works.

📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary

A few words worth knowing before you read.

Anti-Federalist (AN-tee FED-er-uh-list)
A person who opposed the Constitution as written because it gave too much power to the national government.
Federalist (FED-er-uh-list)
A supporter of the new Constitution in 1787 and 1788.
precedent (PRES-ih-dent)
An earlier court decision that guides later decisions.
rule of law (ROOL uv LAW)
The principle that everyone, including the government, must obey the law and that no one is above it.
tyranny (TIR-uh-nee)
Cruel or unfair rule by a person or group with too much power.

Federalists and Anti-Federalists

The Constitution said that it would take effect when nine of the thirteen states approved it. Each state held a special convention for the vote. Supporters of the Constitution called themselves FederalistsFED-er-uh-listA supporter of the new Constitution in 1787 and 1788.. They argued that the Articles had failed and that only a stronger national government could save the Union. Three of them, Alexander Hamilton, James Madison, and John Jay, wrote eighty-five newspaper essays under the name “Publius.” We now call these essays the FederalistFED-er-uh-listA supporter of the new Constitution in 1787 and 1788. Papers.

Their opponents, the Anti-FederalistsAN-tee FED-er-uh-listA person who opposed the Constitution as written because it gave too much power to the national government., feared that the new government would grow too strong. Some wrote under names such as “Brutus” and “the Federal Farmer.” They worried that a national government far from home would become a tyrannyTIR-uh-neeCruel or unfair rule by a person or group with too much power., as the British Crown had. They pointed out that the Constitution had no list of the rights of individual citizens. State constitutions did, they said, and the national one should too. FederalistsFED-er-uh-listA supporter of the new Constitution in 1787 and 1788. answered that a national government with only listed powers could not violate rights it had no power to touch. Many Americans did not find that answer comforting.

Margins That Thin

The first states to approve were Delaware, Pennsylvania, New Jersey, Georgia, and Connecticut, some by large margins. But the larger and more divided states were closer. In Massachusetts the vote in February 1788 was 187 to 168. John Hancock helped win the FederalistsFED-er-uh-listA supporter of the new Constitution in 1787 and 1788. the votes they needed by backing a plan to propose amendments afterward. On June 21, 1788, New Hampshire became the ninth state, and the Constitution could now take effect.

But a Union without Virginia and New York would have been shaky at best. Virginia approved on June 25 by a vote of 89 to 79. New York followed in July by just 30 to 27. Any one of these three states could have said no, and each almost did. If they had, the nation might have split, or the Convention might have been called again. What carried the day was a promise. The FederalistsFED-er-uh-listA supporter of the new Constitution in 1787 and 1788. agreed that the first Congress would take up a bill of rights.

📚 PART 2 · Keeping the Promise12 min

The Bill of Rights

The first Congress kept the promise. James Madison, who had once thought a bill of rights was unnecessary, wrote a list of amendments in 1789 after listening to his neighbors in Virginia. Congress sent twelve amendments to the states. Ten were approved by December 15, 1791, and they are called the Bill of Rights. They protect freedoms such as speech, religion, and a fair trial. You will study them closely in a later lesson. The Anti-FederalistsAN-tee FED-er-uh-listA person who opposed the Constitution as written because it gave too much power to the national government. lost the argument over the Constitution, but they won a lasting gift for the country.

The Rule of Law

What do all these documents have in common? They aim at the rule of lawROOL uv LAWThe principle that everyone, including the government, must obey the law and that no one is above it.. This means that everyone, from the poorest citizen to the President, must obey the law, and that no one can punish people except by the law. The idea is old. The Romans carved it into the Twelve Tables, and English barons forced it on King John in Magna Carta. In the American system it appears in the due process clauses of the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments. They say that a government may not take a person’s life, liberty, or property without fair and regular legal steps.

📚 PART 3 · Where Law Comes From12 min

Four Sources of Law

Where does law come from? In the United States there are four main sources. The first is the Constitution, the highest law of the land. No other law may contradict it. Federal and state constitutions both exist, and each has power in its own sphere. The second source is statutes, laws passed by Congress or by state legislatures. The third is regulations, rules made by agencies of the executive branch, such as the Environmental Protection Agency, to carry out statutes. The fourth is case law, the decisions of judges. When a court decides a case, that decision becomes a precedentPRES-ih-dentAn earlier court decision that guides later decisions., and judges will usually follow it later in similar cases.

A speed limit shows all four at work. A state legislature passes a statute setting the limit. A transportation agency writes regulations about how signs must be posted. A judge, deciding a case about a ticket, adds to the case law. And all of it must stay within the state and federal constitutions. A single rule you meet on the road has passed through every layer.

Two Kinds of Cases

There are also two main types of law. Criminal law deals with crimes, which are wrongs against the whole community, such as theft or assault. In a criminal case the government, acting for the people, brings the charge, and the penalty may be prison. Civil law deals with disputes between people or groups, such as a quarrel over a contract or a damaged car. In a civil case one side sues another, and the usual result is money or an order to act.

Notice how these pieces fit together. The Constitution is on top. Statutes must follow it, regulations must follow statutes, and judges decide cases under all of them. The same government is bound by the same rules as its citizens. That is why the ratification argument still matters. FederalistsFED-er-uh-listA supporter of the new Constitution in 1787 and 1788. and Anti-FederalistsAN-tee FED-er-uh-listA person who opposed the Constitution as written because it gave too much power to the national government. were arguing about how to make power strong enough to rule and yet bound by law, and every citizen inherits the question.
