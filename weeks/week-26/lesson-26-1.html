📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
What happens when a government tries to plan everything?
In the 1900s two huge countries put the whole economy under government control. This lesson looks at what their leaders promised, what they did, and what it cost.

📚 PART 1 · The Promise and the Soviet Plan14 min
📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary

A few words worth knowing before you read.

collectivization (kuh-lek-tiv-ih-ZAY-shun)
The forcing of farmers to give up their land and animals and work on large farms run by the state.
command economy (kuh-MAND ih-KON-uh-mee)
An economy in which the government decides what is made, how much, and at what price.
communism (KOM-yuh-niz-um)
A system in which the government, led by one party, owns the farms and factories and controls the economy.
famine (FAM-in)
A severe shortage of food that causes widespread hunger and death.
Five-Year Plan (FYV-year PLAN)
In the Soviet Union, a government plan that set output goals for every factory and farm for a five-year period.
private property (PRY-vit PROP-er-tee)
Land, homes, businesses, and goods that belong to individuals instead of to the government.

What the Manifesto Promised

In 1848 two Germans, Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels, wrote a short book called The CommunistKOM-yuh-niz-umA system in which the government, led by one party, owns the farms and factories and controls the economy. Manifesto. They said that factory owners and workers were locked in a struggle. They said that workers would one day take over the factories and farms and end the difference between rich and poor. The new society, they promised, would have no classes and no need for a government to rule over anyone. It was a bold promise, and it won followers around the world.

You have studied what communismKOM-yuh-niz-umA system in which the government, led by one party, owns the farms and factories and controls the economy. meant in practice. The leaders who followed Marx did not make the government fade away. They made it all-powerful. They ended private propertyPRY-vit PROP-er-teeLand, homes, businesses, and goods that belong to individuals instead of to the government. in farms and factories and put the economy under government control. This is the command economykuh-MAND ih-KON-uh-meeAn economy in which the government decides what is made, how much, and at what price. you met earlier, in its most complete form.

The Soviet Command Economy

After the Communists took power in Russia in 1917, the Soviet Union slowly built such an economy. A state agency named Gosplan wrote plans for the whole country. In 1928 Joseph Stalin launched the first Five-Year PlanFYV-year PLANIn the Soviet Union, a government plan that set output goals for every factory and farm for a five-year period.. It set goals for every steel mill, coal mine, and farm. The goals were high, and officials who missed them were in danger.

Stalin also ordered collectivizationkuh-lek-tiv-ih-ZAY-shunThe forcing of farmers to give up their land and animals and work on large farms run by the state.. Farmers were forced to give up their land, animals, and tools and join large state-run farms. Many resisted. Some killed their animals instead of handing them over. The state seized grain to feed the cities and to sell abroad, even when the harvest was poor. In 1932 and 1933 a terrible famineFAM-inA severe shortage of food that causes widespread hunger and death. struck, especially in Ukraine. Millions of people starved. The government hid the facts and blocked reporters. A Welsh journalist named Gareth Jones, whom you met earlier in the course, went to see for himself and told the world what he found.

The Soviet Union did build heavy industry. It also built a system in which a citizen could not choose a job freely, start a business, read a free newspaper, or vote in a real election. Prices did not come from buyers and sellers. They were set by officials. Without prices, planners could not tell which goods people really wanted, and stores often stood half empty.

📚 PART 2 · China and the Question of Reform14 min

Why did a planned economy fail so badly at making ordinary goods? Think about what a planner would have to know. In a market, a shoemaker sees that customers want more sandals and fewer boots, and changes what he makes. A central planner in a distant office has to guess how many left shoes, right shoes, and shoelaces a hundred million people need, and then order every factory to make them. Prices would have told the planner what people wanted, but the planner had set the prices. Shortages and waste were the result. Soviet citizens spent hours in lines for bread and shoes, while some factories piled up goods no one wanted.

Mao’s Great Leap

China became a CommunistKOM-yuh-niz-umA system in which the government, led by one party, owns the farms and factories and controls the economy. country in 1949 under Mao Zedong. At first it copied the Soviet model. In 1958 Mao launched the Great Leap Forward. He wanted to turn China into an industrial power in a few years. Farmers were gathered into huge communes. Many were told to make steel in backyard furnaces, and some melted down their pots and tools to do it.

Meanwhile, local officials were afraid to report bad news. They reported huge harvests that did not exist, and the state took grain based on those false numbers. Fields were neglected, and food ran out. From 1958 to 1962 China suffered one of the worst faminesFAM-inA severe shortage of food that causes widespread hunger and death. in history. No one knows how many died. Scholars’ estimates range from about fifteen million to more than forty million people. A free press or an election might have let the truth out sooner. A command economykuh-MAND ih-KON-uh-meeAn economy in which the government decides what is made, how much, and at what price. had no such warning system.

The Complication

The story does not end there. After Mao died in 1976, a new leader, Deng Xiaoping, took China in a different direction. From 1978 on, farm families were allowed to keep what they grew beyond the government’s share, and they could sell it. Special zones were opened to foreign business, and private firms were allowed to grow. China’s economy then grew rapidly, and hundreds of millions of people rose out of extreme poverty.

What does this prove? Honest historians point to two lessons. The first is that when China loosened its control and let markets work, incomes went up. That supports the case that markets create wealth better than plans do. The second is that China did not become a free republic. The CommunistKOM-yuh-niz-umA system in which the government, led by one party, owns the farms and factories and controls the economy. Party still holds all power. There is no free vote and no free press, and the government watches its citizens closely. So a market can be added without freedom following.

👥 Peoples: Russians, Ukrainians, and Chinese

The Soviet Union held many peoples, among them Russians, Ukrainians, Kazakhs, and Georgians. Ukrainians suffered most in the famine of 1932 and 1933. China is home to the Han Chinese, who are the great majority, and to dozens of other peoples, among them Tibetans, Uyghurs, and Mongols.

Think back to Hamilton and Jefferson. Both of them wanted citizens who could stand on their own. A farmer who owns land, and a worker who can change jobs, have power that a person who works only at the state’s direction does not. In a command economykuh-MAND ih-KON-uh-meeAn economy in which the government decides what is made, how much, and at what price., the same government that owns the farm and the factory also owns the newspaper and counts the votes. No one is left who is free of it. That is why the classical writers warned against a government that holds every form of power at once.

A republic judges a system by more than its output. The classical test asks whether there are stable offices, regular law, a way to settle conflict peacefully, and citizens who can speak. By that test, both the Soviet Union and Mao’s China failed their people, even when their factories made goods. Next, you will measure the results with numbers.
