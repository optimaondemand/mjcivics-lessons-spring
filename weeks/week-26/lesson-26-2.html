📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary

A few words worth knowing before you read.

command economy (kuh-MAND ih-KON-uh-mee)
An economy in which the government decides what is made, how much, and at what price.
gross domestic product (GDP) (GROHS duh-MES-tik PROD-ukt)
The total value of all the goods and services a country produces in one year.
incentive (in-SEN-tiv)
Something that makes a person want to act, such as the chance to earn a reward.
per capita (per KAP-ih-tuh)
Latin for “by heads.” Per person.
standard of living (STAN-durd uv LIV-ing)
How well people in a place live, judged by things such as their income, food, housing, and health.

GDP and GDP Per Capita

To compare how people live, economists use a number called gross domestic productGROHS duh-MES-tik PROD-uktThe total value of all the goods and services a country produces in one year., or GDPGROHS duh-MES-tik PROD-uktThe total value of all the goods and services a country produces in one year.. It is the total value of everything a country’s workers and businesses make and sell in a year. A country with a large GDPGROHS duh-MES-tik PROD-uktThe total value of all the goods and services a country produces in one year. produces a lot.

But a large country can have a large GDPGROHS duh-MES-tik PROD-uktThe total value of all the goods and services a country produces in one year. and still be poor. Think of two pizza parties. One has a hundred guests and the other has ten. The first party has more pizza, but each guest may get only a thin slice. To compare fairly, you divide by the number of people. That gives GDPGROHS duh-MES-tik PROD-uktThe total value of all the goods and services a country produces in one year. per capitaper KAP-ih-tuhLatin for “by heads.” Per person., which means per person. It is the most common way to compare a standard of livingSTAN-durd uv LIV-ingHow well people in a place live, judged by things such as their income, food, housing, and health..

The number has limits. It is an average, so it does not show how wealth is shared. It does not count unpaid work, such as caring for a family. It does not measure free time, clean air, or whether people are free to speak and vote. It is a useful tool, but it is only one tool. A citizen should treat the number as evidence, not as the whole story.

Other measures can add to the picture. Economists also look at how long people live, how many can read, and how many children survive to adulthood. In general, the countries with high GDPGROHS duh-MES-tik PROD-uktThe total value of all the goods and services a country produces in one year. per capitaper KAP-ih-tuhLatin for “by heads.” Per person. also do well on these other measures, though not every time. Using several measures together gives a more honest picture than any one number can.

Two Natural Experiments

After World War II, two nations were divided, and the divisions work like experiments. The people on each side shared a language, a history, and a culture. What changed was the system.

Germany was split in 1945 among the winners. West Germany became a democracy with a market economy. East Germany became a Communist state with a command economykuh-MAND ih-KON-uh-meeAn economy in which the government decides what is made, how much, and at what price.. The city of Berlin was split as well, and in 1961 East Germany built a wall to keep its own people from leaving. In the West, people could choose their jobs, buy cars, and travel. In the East, families often waited ten years or more for a small car. By the time the wall opened in 1989, the West was much richer.

Korea was split in the same way. In 1945 Soviet forces held the land north of a line called the 38th parallel, and American forces held the south. A war from 1950 to 1953 left the line in place. South Korea built a market economy, and in time it became a democracy. North Korea built a command economykuh-MAND ih-KON-uh-meeAn economy in which the government decides what is made, how much, and at what price. under one ruling family. In 2023 South Korea’s output was about thirty-three thousand dollars per person. North Korea does not publish reliable numbers, but the Bank of Korea has estimated that its income per person is about one thirtieth of the South’s.

      Comparing Countries Today

      The same pattern shows up in other places. By World Bank figures for 2023, the United States produced about eighty-one thousand dollars per person. Germany produced about fifty-three thousand. China, which has grown quickly since its reforms but is still far less rich per person, produced about twelve and a half thousand. Figures are rounded, and different sources give slightly different numbers, but the gaps are very large.

      Why It Happens

      Economists point to several reasons. The first is incentivesin-SEN-tivSomething that makes a person want to act, such as the chance to earn a reward.. A person who may keep the reward for hard work, or for a good idea, works harder and tries more things. The second is prices. They carry information, telling producers what is scarce and what is wanted. The third is property. People take care of what they own and will invest in it if they know that no one can take it away. The fourth is the rule of law. Contracts are kept, courts are fair, and officials cannot seize what they like.

      Notice that these are the same things a republic needs. A free market and a free republic grow from the same roots, which are law, property, and the freedom to choose. That is the argument President Ronald Reagan made in 1987. Standing at the Berlin Wall, he told the Soviet leader, Mikhail Gorbachev, “Tear down this wall!” He argued that freedom and prosperity go together. The wall came down in 1989, and the Soviet Union itself broke apart in 1991.

      What a Market Cannot Do Alone

      This does not mean that a market can do everything. You saw earlier in this module that markets need rules. A market will not protect itself from monopolies. It will not by itself provide national defense or keep the air clean, and it cannot make sure that every family has a fair start. Citizens of a republic have to decide how far government should go to cover these needs. A mixed economy is the American way of answering that question.

      So the lesson of the twentieth century is not that government has no part to play. It is that when a government takes over the whole economy, it destroys the freedom and the information that make wealth possible, and the people pay for it. Now you can argue the question in the discussion with evidence, rather than opinion.
