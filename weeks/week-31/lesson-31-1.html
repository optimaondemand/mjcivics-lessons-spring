📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Why did the Founders split the government into three parts?
Madison had a hard problem. A government must be strong enough to rule and weak enough to be restrained. His answer was to divide power into three branches, each with its own job.

📚 PART 1 · Article I: Congress Makes the Laws13 min

The Problem Madison Faced

The Founders had learned two lessons. The Articles of Confederation had a government too weak to act. The British Crown had a government too strong to resist. James Madison wanted something in between: a government with real power, split into parts so that no single person or group could use it all. The Constitution does this in its first three articles, each of which sets up one branch.

Congress

Article I creates Congress, the branch that makes laws. Congress is bicameralby-KAM-er-ulHaving two houses, or chambers, as Congress does., with two houses. The House of Representatives has 435 members, divided among the states by population. Representatives serve two-year terms and must be at least twenty-five years old. The Senate has 100 members, two from each state, who serve six-year terms and must be at least thirty. The Framers meant the House to be close to the people and the Senate to be slower and steadier.

The Constitution does not give Congress unlimited power. It lists the enumerated powersih-NOO-mer-ay-ted POW-erzThe powers that the Constitution lists and gives to Congress. in Article I, Section 8. Congress may tax and borrow money, regulate trade, coin money, declare war, raise an army and a navy, and set rules for becoming a citizen. At the end of the list comes the Necessary and Proper Clause, which lets Congress make the laws needed to carry out those powers. This clause has been used many times to stretch what Congress can do, and you will see a famous case about it soon.

Here is how a bill becomes law. A member of Congress introduces a bill. A committee studies it, changes it, and votes. If the committee approves, the whole house debates and votes. The other house must then pass the same bill. Finally it goes to the President, who may sign it or vetoVEE-tohThe President’s power to refuse to sign a bill, which stops it from becoming law unless Congress overrides. it. If the President vetoesVEE-tohThe President’s power to refuse to sign a bill, which stops it from becoming law unless Congress overrides. it, Congress may pass it anyway with a two-thirds vote in each house.

Congress also has the power of impeachmentim-PEECHTo accuse a government official of serious wrongdoing, as a first step toward removing that person from office.. The House can accuse the President, a judge, or another official of serious wrongdoing by a simple majority vote. Then the Senate holds a trial. Two thirds of the senators must vote to convict before the official is removed from office.

📚 PART 2 · Articles II and III: The Executive and the Courts13 min

The President

Article II creates the executive branch, which carries out the laws. The President must be at least thirty-five years old, a natural-born citizen, and a resident of the country for fourteen years. The President serves a four-year term. The powers are wide but limited. The President is commander in chief of the armed forces, makes treaties with the advice and consent of two thirds of the Senate, and appoints ambassadors, judges, and many officials, with the Senate’s approval. The President can grant pardons and can vetoVEE-tohThe President’s power to refuse to sign a bill, which stops it from becoming law unless Congress overrides. bills. The President must also report to Congress on the state of the nation from time to time.

The Framers feared a king. They gave the President enough power to act quickly, especially in war, but they made many of the major powers depend on the Senate or Congress. A President cannot declare war, because that power belongs to Congress. A President cannot raise a tax, because Congress controls money.

The Courts

Article III creates the judicial branch, which judges disputes under the law. It sets up one Supreme Court and lets Congress create lower courts. Federal judges are appointed by the President and approved by the Senate. They hold their offices “during good behavior,” which usually means for life, unless they are impeachedim-PEECHTo accuse a government official of serious wrongdoing, as a first step toward removing that person from office. and convicted. This is meant to free judges from pressure, so that they can rule by the law and not by what is popular.

The courts also have a power not named in the Constitution, called judicial reviewjoo-DISH-ul rih-VYOOThe power of courts to decide whether a law or action fits the Constitution.. It is the power to decide that a law or an action of the government breaks the Constitution and is therefore void. You will see in the next lesson how the Court first claimed it.

📚 PART 3 · Why Three?12 min

Old Ideas Rebuilt

Why did the Founders choose exactly three branches? They followed two thinkers whom you have already studied. Polybius had described Rome’s balance among consuls, Senate, and people, and said that each part restrained the others. Montesquieu, the French writer, said that liberty is lost when the power to make laws, carry them out, and judge them is held by the same hands. He saw these as three different jobs.

The Founders took these ideas and rebuilt them for a republic with no king and no nobles. The legislature came from the people, so the House answered to the voters and the Senate answered to the states. The executive was a single President chosen for a short term. The courts were shielded from the voters by life tenure. Each branch drew its authority from a different source, so none could easily take over the others.

Think back to Rome. The Republic fell when one branch of the government, the generals, became stronger than the law. The Founders asked how to prevent that. Their answer was to give each branch jobs, and also the means to defend its jobs. This is why a bill needs both houses and a signature, why judges are protected, and why the President depends on the Senate. The system is slower than a king’s, and that is partly the point.

Dividing the jobs among three branches was only half of the plan. The other half was to give each branch tools to limit the others. That plan, and the argument for it, is the subject of the next lesson.
