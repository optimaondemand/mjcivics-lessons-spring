📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
How does the Constitution keep each branch from taking too much power?
Dividing the jobs was not enough. Each branch also needed a way to stop the others. This lesson looks at those checks, at the case that created judicial review, and at Madison’s own explanation.

A few words worth knowing before you read.

impeach (im-PEECH)
To accuse a government official of serious wrongdoing, as a first step toward removing that person from office.
judicial review (joo-DISH-ul rih-VYOO)
The power of courts to decide whether a law or action fits the Constitution.
override (oh-ver-RIDE)
To pass a law despite the President’s veto, which takes a two-thirds vote in both houses of Congress.
unconstitutional (un-kon-stih-TOO-shun-ul)
Not allowed by the Constitution.
veto (VEE-toh)
The President’s power to refuse to sign a bill, which stops it from becoming law unless Congress overrides.

Each Branch Holds a Key

Recall the two ideas that students often confuse. Separation of powers divides the jobs among Congress, the President, and the courts. Checks and balances gives each branch the power to limit the others. Here are the main checks, branch by branch.

Congress checks the President in several ways. The Senate must approve many appointments, including judges and ambassadors. Two thirds of the Senate must approve a treaty. And Congress can use impeachmentim-PEECHTo accuse a government official of serious wrongdoing, as a first step toward removing that person from office. to remove a President or a judge. Congress also holds the purse, so no President can spend money without its approval.

The President checks Congress with the vetoVEE-tohThe President’s power to refuse to sign a bill, which stops it from becoming law unless Congress overrides.. A bill that the President dislikes does not become law unless two thirds of both houses vote to pass it again. The President also checks the courts by choosing federal judges.

The courts check both of the others with judicial reviewjoo-DISH-ul rih-VYOOThe power of courts to decide whether a law or action fits the Constitution.. When a case comes before them, judges can declare a law of Congress or an action of the President unconstitutionalun-kon-stih-TOO-shun-ulNot allowed by the Constitution.. The courts in turn depend on the other branches. The President appoints the judges, the Senate confirms them, and Congress decides how many lower courts exist.

Madison explained the purpose of these checks in the Federalist Papers. “If men were angels,” he wrote, “no government would be necessary.” But men are not angels, and so the government must be arranged so that “ambition must be made to counteract ambition.” Each branch has leaders who want to protect their own power. The Founders used that very ambition to guard the Constitution. If one branch reaches for too much, the others have both the means and the motive to push back.

📚 PART 2 · Marbury v. Madison14 min

A Quarrel Over Commissions

In the election of 1800, Thomas Jefferson defeated President John Adams. In the last days before he left office, Adams and the Federalists in Congress created many new judgeships and filled them with Federalists. One of these appointees was William Marbury. His commission, the paper that made his appointment official, was signed, but it was never delivered. Jefferson took office, and his Secretary of State, James Madison, refused to hand it over.

Marbury sued in the Supreme Court. The Chief Justice was John Marshall, who had been Adams’s own Secretary of State and who was a Federalist. Marshall’s choices were all dangerous. If he ordered Madison to deliver the commission, Jefferson might simply refuse, and the Court would look powerless. If he said that Marbury had no case, he would give the Federalists’ opponents an easy victory and look as though he had given way.

Marshall found a third path. In 1803 he wrote that Marbury did have a right to his commission and that Madison had been wrong to withhold it. But the Court could not order it. Marbury had sued under a part of a law of 1789 that gave the Supreme Court a power the Constitution had not given it. That part of the law, Marshall said, was unconstitutionalun-kon-stih-TOO-shun-ulNot allowed by the Constitution.. And “it is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is.”

Marshall had lost the small battle and won a much larger one. The Court could not be ordered around, because it had refused to give an order. Jefferson could not defy a ruling that had given him what he wanted. And the Court had claimed the power of judicial reviewjoo-DISH-ul rih-VYOOThe power of courts to decide whether a law or action fits the Constitution., the power to strike down laws that conflict with the Constitution. It was not used again against a federal law for more than fifty years, but it has shaped American government ever since.

📚 PART 3 · The Limits on Government12 min

Four Ways the Constitution Holds Government Back

The branches do not act alone to keep the government in check. The Constitution limits government in four main ways. The first is the separation of powers and the checks you have just studied. The second is individual rights. The Bill of Rights names freedoms such as speech and religion that no branch may touch.

The third is the rule of law. No official is above the law, and the law applies to all citizens equally. A President who breaks the law can be impeached, and an official who violates a citizen’s rights can be taken to court. The fourth is due process. Before the government takes a person’s life, liberty, or property, it must follow fair steps set by law, such as a fair trial and notice of the charges.

These limits have had an effect far beyond America. Many nations have borrowed pieces of the American plan, such as written constitutions, a separate court system, and bills of rights. In the 1800s, several republics in Latin America copied the idea of dividing power among three branches. Other countries have made their own versions, adapting the ideas to their own history.

Do these limits still work today? That is the question you will discuss with your classmates. As you think about it, remember what Madison said. The checks do not work by themselves. They work because ambitious people in each branch have a reason to defend their own power, and because citizens pay attention. A system of checks is only as strong as the people who use them.
