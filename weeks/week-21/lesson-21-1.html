Dividing the seats in the House of Representatives among the states by population.

A count of everyone living in the country, taken every ten years.
natural-born citizen (NACH-ur-ul born)
A person who was a citizen from birth.
primary election (PRY-mair-ee ee-LEK-shun)
A vote within a party to choose who will be its candidate in the main election.
register (REJ-ih-ster)
To put your name on the official list of people who may vote.

Three Levels of Office

Americans vote for leaders at three levels. At the local level, they choose mayors, city councils, county commissions, and school boards. At the state level, they choose a governor and a state legislature. At the national level, they choose a President, two Senators, and a member of the House of Representatives. A citizen may vote for dozens of offices in a lifetime. Senators were once chosen by state legislatures. The 17th Amendment, ratified in 1913, gave that choice to the voters.

The local level is the one that touches daily life. A school board decides what your school can afford. A city council decides where a road will go. Local elections often have the fewest voters, yet the people elected can change your life sooner than a President can.

Most people are surprised to learn who runs these elections. It is not the President. Under the Elections Clause in Article I, Section 4, each state decides the “times, places and manner” of holding elections for Congress. Congress may step in and change the rules. In practice, states and local officials run nearly every election. The Framers did this on purpose. They did not want one central power to control how the people choose their rulers. Amendments protect who may vote, but states still set many details, such as polling places, hours, and whether voters may vote early or by mail.

Who May Hold Office

The Constitution sets a few simple rules for the highest offices. A member of the House must be at least 25 years old and a citizen for seven years. A Senator must be at least 30 and a citizen for nine years. A President must be at least 35, a natural-born citizenNACH-ur-ul bornA person who was a citizen from birth., and a resident of the United States for fourteen years.

Why different ages? The Framers thought the Senate should be a steady, experienced body. They thought the President needed even more years behind him. The rules about citizenship connect to what you learned about who counts as a citizen. A republic wants its leaders to have a long connection to it. The rule that a President be natural-bornNACH-ur-ul bornA person who was a citizen from birth. came from a fear of foreign influence. The Framers did not want a foreign prince to win the office.

The Steps of an Election

An election has several steps. First, a citizen must registerREJ-ih-sterTo put your name on the official list of people who may vote., which means putting his or her name on the voting list. Most states require this ahead of time. Second, the parties hold primary electionsPRY-mair-ee ee-LEK-shunA vote within a party to choose who will be its candidate in the main election. to choose their candidates. Third comes the general election, when voters pick among the candidates. Fourth, officials count the ballots and announce the winner. American voting is secret. Nobody may force you to show how you voted, so no boss or neighbor can punish you for your choice. The losing side is expected to accept an honest count and try again next time.

Each step is a place where a mistake or a trick could change the result. That is why a republic needs honest officials and watchful citizens. A fair count is not an accident. It is a habit that many people keep every election day. Ordinary citizens also work at the polls, check names on the list, and count ballots. A citizen who signs up as a poll worker is taking a direct part in the republic.

Counting the People

How many seats does a state get in the House? The Constitution answers in Article I, Section 2. Seats are divided by population, and the people are counted every ten years in a censusSEN-susA count of everyone living in the country, taken every ten years.. After each censusSEN-susA count of everyone living in the country, taken every ten years., Congress divides the seats among the states again. This is called apportionmentuh-POR-shun-mentDividing the seats in the House of Representatives among the states by population.. The first censusSEN-susA count of everyone living in the country, taken every ten years. was taken in 1790 and counted about 3.9 million people. The censusSEN-susA count of everyone living in the country, taken every ten years. counts everyone who lives in the country, not only citizens.

The House could have kept growing as the country did. Instead, in 1929, Congress fixed it at 435 seats. After that, a state can gain a seat only if another state loses one. Each censusSEN-susA count of everyone living in the country, taken every ten years. starts a new contest over a limited number of chairs. States whose population grows more slowly than the nation’s lose power in the House.

Geography Moves the Seats

Geography is the reason the contest matters. For much of the last century, Americans moved from the Northeast and Midwest toward the South and West. Jobs, warm weather, and new cities pulled them. The seats moved with them. In 1950 Florida had eight seats in the House. Today it has twenty-eight.

Seats in the House also decide votes in the Electoral College, which you will study in the next lesson. When a state gains people, it gains a voice twice. That is why the censusSEN-susA count of everyone living in the country, taken every ten years. is more than a head count. It is a map of power that is redrawn every ten years.

Think about what this means for you. The same rules that count and divide the people also depend on the people to answer. Each ten years, citizens decide how the republic sees itself by taking part in a count.
