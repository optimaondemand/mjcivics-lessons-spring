📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
What happens when the people choose and the rules fail to produce a winner?
In 1800 the nation had two candidates tied for the presidency, and the Constitution had no clear way to break the tie. The republic did not break. This lesson asks why.

📚 PART 1 · The Electoral College and the Tie13 min
📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary

A few words worth knowing before you read.

elector (ih-LEK-tor)
A person chosen to cast a state’s vote for President.
inauguration (ih-naw-gyuh-RAY-shun)
The ceremony in which a new President takes office.
majority (muh-JOR-ih-tee)
More than half.
transfer of power (TRANS-fur uv POW-ur)

Why Electors?

The Framers did not let voters choose the President directly. Instead, each state chooses electorsih-LEK-torA person chosen to cast a state’s vote for President., and the electorsih-LEK-torA person chosen to cast a state’s vote for President. choose the President. A state has as many electorsih-LEK-torA person chosen to cast a state’s vote for President. as it has Representatives and Senators together. Today there are 538 electorsih-LEK-torA person chosen to cast a state’s vote for President. in all. That number is 435 Representatives, 100 Senators, and 3 electorsih-LEK-torA person chosen to cast a state’s vote for President. for Washington, D.C. A candidate needs a majoritymuh-JOR-ih-teeMore than half., which is 270 votes, to win.

Alexander Hamilton explained the reason in Federalist No. 68. He argued that a small group of electorsih-LEK-torA person chosen to cast a state’s vote for President., picked by the people, would judge better than a crowd. They would be harder to stir up by anger or tricks. The system was meant to keep the highest office out of the hands of a demagogue who could inflame the people. The Framers had seen how a clever speaker could lead a crowd, and they wanted a check on that danger.

Each state decides how its electorsih-LEK-torA person chosen to cast a state’s vote for President. are chosen. Today every state lets its voters pick them. In all but two states, Maine and Nebraska, the candidate who wins the most votes in the state gets all of its electorsih-LEK-torA person chosen to cast a state’s vote for President.. A candidate can win the most votes across the whole country and still lose in the Electoral College, and that has happened a few times.

A Tie Nobody Planned For

The Framers did not expect political parties. By 1800, though, there were two. The Federalists backed John Adams. The campaign was bitter. Federalists said Jefferson would bring chaos. Republicans called Adams a tyrant. The country was also nervous about a possible war with France, and you will see how that fear led to a harsh law. The Republicans backed Thomas Jefferson, with Aaron Burr of New York as his running mate. At that time, each electorih-LEK-torA person chosen to cast a state’s vote for President. cast two votes, and the top vote-getter became President. The runner-up became Vice President. Nobody said which vote was for which job.

The Republicans won. But every one of their electorsih-LEK-torA person chosen to cast a state’s vote for President. voted for both Jefferson and Burr, so the two men tied with 73 votes each. Adams had 65. Burr could have said at once that Jefferson was the choice. He stayed quiet, and that kept the contest alive. The Constitution said that a tie went to the House of Representatives, where each state casts one vote. A candidate needed a majoritymuh-JOR-ih-teeMore than half. of the states. The House was full of Federalists who had just lost, and they could choose between two men they did not like.

📚 PART 2 · The House Decides13 min

Thirty-Six Ballots

The House began voting in February 1801. Day after day, the count was the same. Jefferson held eight states and Burr held six, and two were split. They voted again and again, thirty-six times in all. The new President had to take office on March 4.

This was dangerous. Suppose the House had not chosen by March 4. Adams’s term would have ended, and the Constitution did not say clearly who would lead the country. There would have been no President. Some Republican leaders talked about using state militias if the Federalists stole the election. Some Federalists talked about making a President by law. The country stood close to a break.

Hamilton disliked Jefferson, but he trusted Burr much less. He wrote letters urging his fellow Federalists to choose Jefferson. He judged that Jefferson, though wrong on many things, was a man of principle. On the thirty-sixth ballot, enough Federalists left their votes blank, and Jefferson was elected on February 17.

The Fix, and the Speech

The nation fixed the problem. The 12th Amendment, ratified in 1804, said that electorsih-LEK-torA person chosen to cast a state’s vote for President. would cast one ballot for President and a separate ballot for Vice President. A tie like that of 1800 could no longer happen by accident. If no one wins a majoritymuh-JOR-ih-teeMore than half., the House still decides among the top candidates, and the Senate chooses the Vice President.

Look at what Hamilton did. He had fought Jefferson for years, and he did not trust him. Still, he put the safety of the republic ahead of his own feelings. That is civic virtue at work. A leader helped the other side win because he feared worse for the country.

🏛️ Cities: Washington, D.C.

The capital had moved to the new city on the Potomac River only a few months before the election. The streets were muddy, and the Capitol was still unfinished. Jefferson took his oath there on March 4, 1801, in the first inauguration held in Washington.

Jefferson gave his inaugural addressih-naw-gyuh-RAY-shunThe ceremony in which a new President takes office. in a calm voice. He said that disagreements over ideas were not the same as disagreements over loyalty. “We are all Republicans, we are all Federalists,” he said. He added, “Every difference of opinion is not a difference of principle.” He wanted to calm the country after a bitter fight.

Compare this with Rome. When Sulla wanted power, he marched his army on the city. When Caesar wanted it, he crossed a river with his soldiers. In America, power moved from one party to the other without a soldier. That transfer of powerTRANS-fur uv POW-urThe handing of power from one leader or party to the next. was not guaranteed. It happened because many people chose to accept the result. President Adams, who had lost, left Washington before the ceremony. He gave up the office without a fight.
